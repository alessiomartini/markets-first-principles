## IN ONE SENTENCE

A bond is a loan you can sell, and its price is the present value of a schedule that was fixed the day it was
issued.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Borrowers want money now against a promise to repay later; lenders want that promise to be transferable. A bank
loan achieves the first and not the second — it sits on the lender's balance sheet until maturity. Standardising
the promise into a security solves the second, and the consequence is enormous: the lender can change their mind,
which makes them willing to lend more cheaply in the first place.

Everything distinctive about bonds follows from the schedule being fixed. Because the payments do not move, the
price moves entirely through the discount rate applied to them and the perceived chance of receiving them. That
makes bonds the cleanest instrument in which to study interest rate risk, and it is why the machinery of Track 5's
macro pages is built here rather than there.

## DEFINITION

A bond promises a **coupon** — a periodic interest payment fixed at issue, quoted as a percentage of face value —
and repayment of principal at maturity. The coupon is set once and says nothing about the return an investor will
earn, which depends on the price paid.

That return is summarised by the **yield to maturity**: the single discount rate which, applied to all remaining
cash flows, reproduces the current price. It is a summary statistic and not a rate anyone receives, since it
assumes every coupon is reinvested at that same rate.

Two derivatives of the price-yield relationship do the practical work. **Duration** is its slope — the percentage
price change per unit change in yield, and equivalently the weighted average time at which cash flows arrive.
**Convexity** is its curvature, the second-order correction, and it is genuinely valuable: prices rise more when
yields fall than they fall when yields rise.

For anything other than a government, the yield contains a **credit spread** over the risk-free curve,
compensating for the possibility of **default** and for being harder to sell. Default is not the same as total
loss — what matters is the recovery rate, the fraction eventually repaid.

## FORMALLY

Price is present value, with a single rate standing in for the whole **zero-coupon curve**:

$$
P(y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{c}{\left(1 + y\right)^{i}} + \frac{F}{\left(1 + y\right)^{n}}
$$

Everything else is a Taylor expansion of this in $y$. Modified duration and convexity are the first two
coefficients,

$$
D_{\text{mod}} = -\frac{1}{P}\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}, \qquad
C = \frac{1}{P}\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial y^2}
$$

so that a yield move $\Delta y$ produces

$$
\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}\,C\,\left(\Delta y\right)^2
$$

Two things are worth reading off this. First, $C > 0$ always for an ordinary bond, so the quadratic term helps in
both directions — a symmetric yield move produces an asymmetric price move in the holder's favour, which is why
convexity is priced. Second, duration alone is a linear approximation whose error grows quadratically, so a
duration hedge that is exact for a basis point leaks money in a hundred, and must be rebalanced. That is the same
structure as delta hedging in Track 4, and for the same reason.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A Taylor expansion, and knowing when to stop">
Duration and convexity are simply the first and second derivatives of price with respect to yield, given trading
names. Recognising that immediately answers questions that look like separate facts: why hedges need rebalancing
(the first derivative is not constant), why convexity is always beneficial for a plain bond (the second derivative
is positive), and why long-dated bonds are more sensitive to everything (both derivatives grow with maturity).

The instructive part is where the expansion stops being adequate. It is an expansion in a *single* variable, so it
assumes the entire **zero-coupon curve** shifts in parallel — which is not how curves move. Real curve changes
have at least three modes, conventionally level, slope and curvature, and a portfolio immunised against a parallel
shift can still lose substantially in a steepening. This is a familiar situation: a one-parameter perturbation
theory that works until the perturbation has structure the parameter cannot represent, at which point the fix is
to expand in the right basis rather than to add more terms in the wrong one.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Coupon is not yield.** The coupon is fixed at issue; the yield moves with the price and is what an investor
  actually earns if they hold to maturity and reinvest at that rate.
- **Yield to maturity is not a forecast.** It embeds a reinvestment assumption that will not hold.
- **Duration is measured in years and is not a horizon.** It is a sensitivity that happens to have units of time,
  which is a coincidence of the algebra rather than a statement about when you get your money.
- **Default is not total loss.** Recovery rates for senior secured debt are often substantial; pricing credit means
  estimating both the probability and what remains.
- **A "risk-free" bond is not free of risk.** It is free of *credit* risk. Its price still moves with rates, and a
  thirty-year government bond is a volatile instrument.
