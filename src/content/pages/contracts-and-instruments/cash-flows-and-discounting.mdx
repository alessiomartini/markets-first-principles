## IN ONE SENTENCE

Every financial instrument is a schedule of payments at future dates, and its price is what those payments are
worth today.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Money now and money later are different goods. A pound today can be lent, invested, or spent; a pound in a year
cannot do any of those things in the meantime, and might not arrive. Any comparison between the two requires an
exchange rate between dates, and that exchange rate is what an interest rate is.

Once that rate exists, valuation becomes mechanical. A bond, a swap, a loan, a lease, a pension liability — all of
them are a list of amounts and dates, and all of them are priced the same way. This is why fixed income is a
single subject rather than a collection of unrelated products, and why the machinery here reappears in every later
track. Track 4 prices options by the same method, having first replaced the payments with their expected values
under a particular probability measure.

## DEFINITION

The **present value** of a future payment is what it is worth today. It is obtained by multiplying the payment by
a **discount factor** — the number that converts an amount at date $T$ into an amount at date $t$.

The set of discount factors for every maturity is the **zero-coupon curve**. It is not directly observable,
because real bonds pay **coupon**s along the way rather than a single amount at maturity, so the curve must be
*bootstrapped* out of the prices of instruments that do trade. Two desks with identical market data can build
slightly different curves, which is why curve construction is a discipline rather than a data-loading step.

Two conventions intrude on the arithmetic and cause more errors than the theory does. **Accrual** is interest
earned but not yet paid: because coupons arrive at discrete dates while interest accrues continuously, a buyer
between coupon dates compensates the seller for the part already earned, which is why the price you pay exceeds
the price that is quoted. The **day count convention** is the rule converting a period between two dates into a
fraction of a year, and it differs by market and instrument with no economic content whatsoever.

## FORMALLY

An instrument paying amounts $c_i$ at dates $T_i$ is worth

$$
V = \sum_i c_i \, D(t, T_i)
$$

and everything else in this page is a statement about $D$. Under continuous compounding with a maturity-dependent
zero rate $y(t,T)$,

$$
D(t,T) = e^{-y(t,T)\,(T - t)}
$$

The curve is bootstrapped forward from the shortest instrument. Given discount factors out to $T_{n-1}$, a bond
maturing at $T_n$ with price $P$ and coupons $c$ determines the next one directly:

$$
D(t,T_n) = \frac{P - \sum_{i<n} c \, D(t,T_i)}{c + 1}
$$

One consistency condition matters more than any other. **Discount factor**s must be decreasing in maturity,
because $D(t,T_1) < D(t,T_2)$ for $T_1 < T_2$ would mean the implied **forward rate** between the two dates is
negative in a way you could exploit: borrow long, lend short, pocket the difference with certainty. A curve that
fails this is not an interesting economic observation but an error in the bootstrap.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A propagator between dates">
The discount factor is a propagator. It carries a quantity from one time to another, it composes multiplicatively
across intermediate times, and it is the object rather than the rate that has clean algebra:

$$
D(t, T_2) = D(t, T_1) \, D(T_1, T_2)
$$

That composition law is why practitioners work in discount factors and quote in rates. A rate is a logarithm of a
propagator divided by an interval, which makes it convenient for talking and awkward for calculating — the moment
you compound across periods you are multiplying propagators and taking logarithms back and forth for no reason.

The analogy also explains why the curve is bootstrapped rather than measured. What is observable is the integrated
response over each instrument's whole life, and recovering the local structure from a set of integrals is an
inverse problem, with the usual consequence that different regularisation choices give different answers in the
gaps between observed maturities.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **The quoted price is not the price you pay.** The difference is **accrual**, and it is the first thing to check
  when a bond calculation is off by a small amount.
- **A yield is not a discount rate you can apply to another bond.** **Yield to maturity** is a per-instrument
  summary; the **zero-coupon curve** is what actually discounts cash flows.
- **Day count conventions have no economic content and still change the numbers.** Two correct implementations
  disagreeing by a few basis points are usually disagreeing about ACT/360 versus ACT/365.
- **Negative rates are not a paradox.** A **discount factor** above one simply means paying to hold money, which
  was ordinary in Europe for most of a decade.

## GO DEEPER

- Hull, *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*, chapter 4. The standard treatment of rates, conventions and
  bootstrapping.
- Tuckman & Serrat, *Fixed Income Securities*, chapters 1–3 — more careful than Hull on exactly the conventions
  that cause errors.
- Shreve, *Stochastic Calculus for Finance II*, chapter 5, for where discounting goes once rates are stochastic.
- Paper: Hagan & West, *Interpolation Methods for Curve Construction* (2006) — a demonstration that the choice of
  interpolation is not innocent, with examples of methods that produce absurd forward rates.
