## IN ONE SENTENCE

A derivative is a contract whose value is entirely a function of the price of something else, so it creates no new
asset and can only move risk between the people who sign it.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Some risks are attached to things that are awkward to trade. A baker is exposed to the price of wheat but has no
use for a warehouse of it; a company with loans at a floating rate is exposed to interest rates, which cannot be
bought at all. A derivative lets the exposure be separated from the object: you take the price risk without taking
the thing, and someone who wants the opposite risk takes the other side.

Two further reasons follow from this. Taking the exposure usually costs far less than buying the **underlying**,
which is what makes derivatives cheap for hedgers and tempting for speculators. And because the contract is only an
agreement between two parties, it can be shaped to fit almost any exposure, which is why so many different kinds
exist.

## DEFINITION

A derivative is a contract between two parties whose payments depend on the price of a reference quantity, the
**underlying**: a share, an index, a commodity, an exchange rate, an interest rate, or even another derivative.
The contract is not the underlying and need not ever involve it.

The most useful way to sort derivatives is by the shape of their **payoff**. A **linear payoff** changes
one-for-one with the underlying: forwards, futures and swaps. A **non-linear payoff** bends: the contract gains
more than it loses, or the reverse, depending on where the underlying ends up, which is the defining property of
options. Linear contracts have a **notional** that sets the size of the payments; options have a strike and an
expiry that set where the bend sits.

Two numbers are constantly confused. The **exposure** of a position is how much it gains or loses per unit move in
the underlying; it is what you actually risk. The **notional** is just the reference amount used to compute the
payments. **Leverage** is the ratio between the exposure you control and the capital you committed: a derivative
usually needs little or no money up front, so a given amount of capital can carry a much larger exposure than
buying the underlying outright.

Every derivative has two sides, and what one party gains the other loses. Summed over all participants, the
derivative itself creates no wealth and no net exposure; what it does is redistribute exposure toward those who
are willing to hold it. The identity of the other side matters whenever there is no clearing house, because the
**counterparty** might not pay.

## FORMALLY

Write the underlying price at the contract's end as $S_T$. A derivative is defined by a payoff function

$$
V_T = f(S_T)
$$

fixed by the contract. For a long forward struck at $K$ the payoff is $f(S_T) = S_T - K$, which is linear in $S_T$.
For a call option struck at $K$ it is $f(S_T) = \max(S_T - K,\, 0)$, which has a kink at $K$.

Exposure is the slope of the value with respect to the underlying,

$$
\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S},
$$

and it is constant for a linear payoff and varies with $S$ for a non-linear one. Leverage can then be written as
the exposure in money terms divided by the capital committed,

$$
\lambda = \frac{\Delta \, S}{\text{capital posted}} .
$$

Nothing in these formulas says that notional appears in the risk. A swap with a huge notional can have a tiny
$\Delta$ if its two legs nearly offset, and that is exactly the case the headline numbers hide.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A response function, not a source">
A derivative behaves like a response function: it does not generate anything of its own, it transforms an input,
the underlying price path, into an output, the payoff. The linear case is a linear response, where doubling the
input doubles the output and a measurement taken at one operating point holds everywhere. The non-linear case is
the regime where the response depends on the operating point, and where superposition fails.

The accounting is also conservation-like. Every derivative has a long and a short side whose payoffs sum to zero,
the way internal forces in a closed system sum to zero. Derivatives therefore move risk around inside the market
without adding any, and the questions worth asking are where it ends up and whether the holders can bear it.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **A very large notional is not a very large risk.** Notional is only the reference amount for computing
  payments, and offsetting positions can reduce the net exposure to a small fraction of it. The scary
  "hundreds of trillions" headlines add up notionals, which is not the same as adding up what could be lost.
- **A derivative is not a bet on the underlying going up.** The same contract is a hedge for one holder and a
  speculation for another; the contract does not know which, and the difference is what else the holder owns.
- **Derivatives do not create money or assets.** For every gain there is an equal loss on the other side, which
  is why the risk to watch is whether the loser can pay, not the size of the contract.
- **Little money up front does not mean little risk.** Low cost of entry is **leverage**, and leverage widens the
  range of outcomes in both directions.
