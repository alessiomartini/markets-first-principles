## IN ONE SENTENCE

A share is what is left over after everyone else with a claim on a company has been paid.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Someone has to absorb the uncertainty. A business's revenues are variable while its obligations — wages, supplies,
interest, tax — are largely fixed, so the difference has to land somewhere. Equity is the instrument invented to
put it in one place, held by people who accepted that position knowingly and can be paid for it.

That arrangement is what makes the rest of the capital structure possible. Because equity absorbs the first
losses, debt can be nearly safe, and because debt is nearly safe it is cheap. A company's financing is a stack of
claims sorted by who takes losses first, and the interesting consequence is that the same underlying business can
be carved into instruments of wildly different risk.

## DEFINITION

A **share** is a unit of ownership: a residual claim on assets and earnings, usually a vote, and no promise of
anything. Its position at the back of the queue is the entire character of the asset — it is why equity has
unlimited upside, why it can go to zero while the company keeps trading under its creditors, and why it is more
volatile than the business underneath it.

Cash reaches shareholders two ways. A **dividend** pays it out directly. A **buyback** retires shares instead, so
each remaining share owns a larger fraction. They are economically close and are treated differently by tax
systems, which is most of why the mix between them has shifted over time.

Three measures of size are routinely confused. **Market capitalisation** is price times shares outstanding — a
marginal price extrapolated to every share, so not an amount anyone could realise. **Free float** is the portion
actually available to trade, excluding strategic holders who never sell. **Enterprise value** adds net debt, which
is the right basis for comparing firms financed differently. An **ADR** is a wrapper letting a foreign share trade
on a US exchange, not a different asset.

## FORMALLY

The residual claim can be written exactly. If the firm's assets are worth $A$ at some horizon and its debt
requires repayment of $D$, then equity holders receive

$$
E = \max\left(A - D,\, 0\right)
$$

because limited liability means they cannot be pursued below zero. That expression is the **payoff** of a **call
option** on the assets with **strike** equal to the debt — an observation due to Merton, and one of the most
productive in finance. It explains immediately why equity value rises with the volatility of the business, why
shareholders of a distressed firm prefer risky projects, and why deeply indebted equity trades like a lottery
ticket rather than like a share of a business.

The other formal point on this page concerns returns. Price return omits **dividend**s; total return reinvests
them:

$$
R_{\text{total}} = \prod_{t}\left(1 + \frac{P_t - P_{t-1} + d_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) - 1
$$

The difference looks small annually and compounds relentlessly, which is what the figure shows. Any analysis
performed on a raw price series is measuring the wrong quantity, and the error grows with the horizon.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A boundary condition that creates convexity">
Limited liability is a boundary condition — the payoff is clipped at zero — and clipping a linear response is what
manufactures convexity out of nothing. This is worth internalising because it recurs everywhere in the subject:
wherever a payoff cannot go below some level, the instrument acquires option-like behaviour, and its value starts
depending on the *variance* of the underlying quantity rather than only on its expectation.

The consequence is a genuine conflict of interest with a clean formal statement. Because $E = \max(A - D, 0)$ is
convex in $A$, Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb{E}[E] > \max(\mathbb{E}[A] - D, 0)$: shareholders of a levered
firm are made better off by an increase in the volatility of the assets, holding the mean fixed. Bondholders, who
hold the complementary concave claim, are made worse off by exactly the same amount. Most of corporate governance
and debt covenants exist to manage that asymmetry, and it is visible in the algebra before it is visible in
anyone's behaviour.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **A dividend is not a return.** The price drops by roughly the amount paid. Only total return measures what a
  shareholder earned.
- **Market cap is not money invested in the company.** Except at issuance, buying shares moves money between
  investors and none of it reaches the firm — the **primary market** versus **secondary market** distinction from
  Track 0.
- **Buybacks do not "return cash to shareholders" more than dividends do.** They deliver it by changing the
  denominator instead of the numerator, which is why earnings per share can rise while earnings do not.
- **A price chart is not a return chart.** Ignoring dividends understates long-run equity performance by a margin
  that grows with the sample.
