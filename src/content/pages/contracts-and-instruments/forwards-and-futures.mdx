## IN ONE SENTENCE

A forward fixes today the price of a transaction that happens later; a future is the same promise, standardised
and settled daily so that nobody has to trust anybody.

## WHY IT EXISTS

A farmer with a crop six months from harvest and a mill that will need flour then have exactly opposite worries
about the price. Both would rather know now. The contract that lets them agree is the oldest derivative there is,
and it exists to transfer **natural exposure** from people who have it involuntarily to people willing to hold it.

The problem with a bilateral promise is that six months is a long time to trust a stranger. If prices move a long
way, the losing side has a large incentive to walk away — and the more useful the contract has been, the larger
that incentive. Futures markets solve this not by improving trust but by removing the need for it: standardise the
contract so anyone can take either side, interpose a **CCP** so you never face your counterparty, and settle the
change in value *every day* so no large claim ever accumulates.

## DEFINITION

The **forward price** is the price agreed now for settlement later. For a storable asset it is not a forecast: it
is pinned by arbitrage to the **spot** price plus the **cost of carry** — financing, **storage** and insurance,
less any income the asset produces. Anyone with a warehouse and a loan can enforce it.

A future is a **standardisation** forward traded on an exchange, and two mechanisms make it a different animal.
**Margin** is collateral posted against the position: *initial* margin at the start, sized to a plausible adverse
move, and *variation* margin exchanged daily. **Mark-to-market** is that daily settlement — the position's change
in value is actually paid, so credit exposure resets to zero each day and a futures position generates cash flows
before it expires, which a forward does not.

Because each contract expires, holding a long-term exposure means a repeated **roll** into later contracts. Its
cost depends on the curve's shape: **contango** is longer-dated above nearer, the normal state for a storable
asset since it merely reflects carry; **backwardation** is the reverse, implying a convenience yield — someone
needs the physical material now badly enough to pay for holding it.

## FORMALLY

The no-arbitrage relation is enforceable rather than assumed. With financing rate $r$, storage cost $u$ and income
yield $q$, all continuous,

$$
F(t,T) = S_t \, e^{\left(r + u - q\right)\left(T - t\right)}
$$

If $F$ exceeded this, you would buy spot, store, and sell forward for a certain profit; if it fell below, the
reverse, provided the asset can be borrowed and sold short. That proviso matters: for many commodities the reverse
trade is impossible, so the relation holds as an inequality in one direction only, and **backwardation** can
persist without being an arbitrage.

The consequence for anyone holding exposure through futures is the roll return. If the curve keeps its shape and
spot is unchanged, rolling from a contract at $F(t,T_1)$ into one at $F(t,T_2)$ earns

$$
r_{\text{roll}} \approx -\left(r + u - q\right)\left(T_2 - T_1\right)
$$

which is *negative* in **contango**. Over years this term dominates the return of commodity index products —
frequently overwhelming the move in the spot price entirely, so that an investor can be right about oil and lose
money holding it through futures. That is the single most consequential fact on this page.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A constraint, not a prediction">
The relation $F = S e^{(r+u-q)\tau}$ is a constraint imposed by the existence of a physical operation — buy,
store, deliver — in the same way a conservation law is imposed by a symmetry rather than derived from dynamics. It
tells you nothing about where the price is going, only which price pairs cannot coexist. A physicist should find
this reassuring and a forecaster should find it disappointing, and the persistent misreading of contango as
bullishness is what happens when the two are confused.

The interesting physics is where the constraint breaks. Short selling asymmetry means the arbitrage is executable
in one direction only, so the relation is a one-sided bound and the market can sit inside it indefinitely.
Storage capacity is finite, so $u$ is not a constant but rises steeply as inventories fill — and diverges when
they are full, which is how crude oil traded below zero in April 2020. A constraint derived by assuming an
unbounded reservoir stops constraining anything at the moment the reservoir fills.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **A futures price is not a forecast of the spot price.** For a storable asset it is spot plus carry, an
  arbitrage identity. This bears repeating because the mistake is nearly universal outside the market.
- **Contango is not bearish and backwardation is not bullish.** They are statements about carry and physical
  tightness, not about direction.
- **Futures and forwards are not identical.** Daily **mark-to-market** means a futures position generates interim
  cash flows, which matters when rates are correlated with the underlying.
- **Margin is not a deposit or a down payment.** It is collateral against loss, and a **margin** call is a demand
  for more of it, not a payment for the asset.
