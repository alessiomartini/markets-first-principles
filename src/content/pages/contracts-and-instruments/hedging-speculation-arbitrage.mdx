## IN ONE SENTENCE

The same trade is a hedge, a speculation or an arbitrage depending on what the trader already owns and on what the
trade is compared with.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Every earlier page on this track described an instrument, and none of them said who should hold it. The answer is
not in the contract. A forward, a future, an option and a swap are only payoffs, and a payoff has no motive.

The motive comes from the rest of the balance sheet. An airline must buy fuel next year at a price it does not
know; a fuel producer must sell it at a price it does not know. Neither chose that risk: it came with the
business. A market in which such firms can pass the risk to one another, or to someone paid to carry it, is what
the derivatives market is for, and the three words in the title name the three kinds of participant that make it
work.

## DEFINITION

A **hedge** is a position taken to offset a risk that is already there. The risk comes from doing business, and is
called a **natural exposure**: the airline is naturally short fuel, since a rise in its price hurts, and the
producer is naturally long. The hedge is the trade that cancels the part of the exposure the firm does not want.
It is chosen for what it removes, not for what it earns.

A speculation is the opposite: a position with no underlying exposure to offset, taken because the trader
expects to be paid for bearing the risk. If the airline buys fuel futures it is hedging. If a fund with no use for
fuel buys the same contract it is speculating. The two trades are identical on the exchange and different in
purpose, which is why the purpose cannot be read from the trade.

An **arbitrage** is neither. It is a set of positions that costs nothing, cannot lose and sometimes gains, so
that it does not depend on a view about the future. Arbitrage is what holds the prices of related instruments
together: the forward price is bound to spot plus the **carry cost**, a call cannot be worth more than the
underlying, and every such limit is an **arbitrage bound**. The speculator takes risk to earn a return; the
arbitrageur takes none, and for that reason ends up enforcing the prices on which the hedger relies.

The three need each other. Hedgers bring a risk they want to shed, speculators accept it for a price, and
arbitrageurs keep the prices of the instruments they use consistent. Without the second group the first has no
counterparty at a fair price; without the third, the contract the hedger buys may not be worth what it appears.

Hedging does not remove risk. It replaces the large risk of the exposure with a smaller one, the **basis risk**:
the contract in use is almost never identical to the thing being protected. An airline hedges jet fuel with crude
or heating oil futures because those contracts are liquid, and is left holding the difference between the two
prices. How much of the contract to use is the **hedge ratio**.

## FORMALLY

Let $\Delta S$ be the change in the price of the exposure over the hedging horizon and $\Delta F$ the change in
the price of the hedging instrument. A position of $h$ units of the instrument against one unit of the exposure
changes in value by

$$
\Delta P = \Delta S - h \, \Delta F .
$$

Choosing $h$ to minimise the variance of $\Delta P$ gives the minimum-variance hedge ratio

$$
h^{*} = \rho \, \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F},
$$

where $\rho$ is the correlation of $\Delta S$ and $\Delta F$ and $\sigma_S$, $\sigma_F$ are their standard
deviations. This is the slope of a regression of $\Delta S$ on $\Delta F$. The variance that remains is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\Delta P) = \sigma_S^{2}\left(1 - \rho^{2}\right),
$$

so a fraction $\rho^{2}$ of the variance is removed and $1-\rho^{2}$ is the basis risk. A perfect hedge needs
$|\rho| = 1$, which requires the instrument to be the exposure.

Two consequences. First, $h^{*}$ is an estimate: $\rho$, $\sigma_S$ and $\sigma_F$ are measured on past data and
they move, often most in the period in which the hedge matters. Second, the arbitrageur's case is the limit in
which the two legs are the same payoff, so that $\rho = 1$ and the residual variance is zero by construction. A
convergence trade between two merely similar instruments has $\rho < 1$ and belongs to the first case, not the
second.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Cancelling a fluctuation, not a field">
A hedge is a compensation: a second term chosen so that the fluctuating part of the first is cancelled, in the way
a common-mode signal is removed by differencing two channels. The cancellation is only as good as the correlation
between the channels. The residual noise is $\sigma_S\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}$, and no choice of $h$ can reduce it
below that.

What the analogy does not give is a free lunch. Cancelling noise costs something here: the hedger pays the
speculator to carry the risk, and pays again when the ratio drifts. Nor is the arbitrageur a damping term in the
physical sense, since arbitrage works only while the arbitrageur has capital, and capital runs out.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **A derivative is not a hedge or a speculation by nature.** The label belongs to the holder's whole position.
  The same short position in crude futures is a hedge for a producer and a bet for a fund.
- **A hedge is not free, and not automatic.** It costs spread, margin and the gains forgone when prices move
  favourably. A firm that hedges and then watches the price move its way has not made a mistake; it has bought
  insurance and not needed it.
- **A hedge is not a guarantee.** Basis risk and a drifting hedge ratio remain. A hedge that looked perfect on
  historical data can fail in the stress episode for which it was bought.
- **"Arbitrage" is used loosely.** A trade that is only likely to converge, such as two related stocks or a bond
  against its swap, can move against the holder for longer than the holder can finance it. It is a speculation
  with a favourable bias, and it can lose.
