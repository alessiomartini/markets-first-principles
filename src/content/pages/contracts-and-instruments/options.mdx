## IN ONE SENTENCE

An option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to trade something at a fixed price, so the holder
keeps the good outcomes and walks away from the bad ones.

## WHY IT EXISTS

A forward fixes a price and binds both sides: if the market moves your way you gain, and if it moves against you
you lose the same amount. Many real exposures are not symmetric. A company bidding for a foreign contract needs
the exchange rate to be right only if it wins the bid; an investor who wants to own a share but fears a crash
wants protection against the fall without giving up the rise.

An option is the contract built for that asymmetry. One party pays a price up front to acquire a choice, and the
other party is paid to take on the obligation. The price of that choice is the central question of derivatives
pricing, and it exists because a choice that can only help its holder cannot be free.

## DEFINITION

A **call option** gives the right to buy the underlying at a fixed price; a **put option** gives the right to sell
it. That fixed price is the **strike**. The right lasts until the **expiry**, after which the contract is worth
nothing. The seller, often called the writer, has the opposite position: an obligation to trade if the holder
asks.

The **exercise style** says when the right can be used. A *European* option can be exercised only at expiry; an
*American* option at any time up to expiry. Which style a contract has depends on the contract, not on the
continent where it trades.

The **payoff** at expiry is what the option is worth then, and it has a kink at the strike. A call pays the
amount by which the underlying exceeds the strike, or nothing if it does not. A put pays the amount by which the
underlying falls below the strike, or nothing. **Moneyness** describes where the current price sits relative to
the strike: an option that would pay if it expired now is in the money, one that would not is out of the money,
and one sitting at the strike is at the money.

The price paid for the option today is the **premium**. It splits into two parts. The **intrinsic value** is what
the option would pay if exercised right now, and it can never be negative. The **time value** is the rest: what
buyers pay for the chance that the underlying moves favourably before expiry. An option far out of the money is
all time value, and that time value fades to zero as expiry approaches if the move never comes.

The buyer's loss is capped at the premium. The writer's position is the mirror image: a small known gain, and a
loss that is large, and for a written call on a rising asset in principle unlimited.

## FORMALLY

For strike $K$ and underlying price $S_T$ at expiry, the payoffs of the long positions are

$$
C_T = \max(S_T - K,\, 0), \qquad P_T = \max(K - S_T,\, 0).
$$

Before expiry the premium decomposes as

$$
\text{premium} = \underbrace{\max(S - K,\, 0)}_{\text{intrinsic value (call)}} + \text{time value},
$$

and time value is non-negative for a European call on an asset that pays no income, because the option can always
be held to expiry rather than exercised early.

Calls and puts on the same underlying, strike and expiry are tied to each other. For European options on an asset
paying no income, with continuous interest rate $r$ and time $\tau$ to expiry,

$$
C - P = S - K e^{-r\tau}.
$$

This is put-call parity. It holds by replication: a long call and a short put have exactly the payoff of a forward
struck at $K$, which is worth the share price minus the discounted strike. It says that once you know the price
of the call, the price of the put is not a separate question.

Notice what the formulas do not contain. They give the payoff at expiry and a relation between two prices, but
they do not say what the premium itself should be. That requires a model of how the underlying moves, which is
the subject of the pricing track.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A kinked potential with a smoothed profile">
The payoff at expiry is a function with a kink: flat on one side of the strike and rising with unit slope on the
other. Before expiry the option price is that kink smoothed out. The more uncertain the final price and the more
time remaining, the wider and rounder the smoothing, in the way a sharp initial profile spreads under diffusion.

This is why the price of an option depends on the width of the distribution of future prices and not only on its
centre, and why the premium is largest at the strike, where the kink is sharpest and the smoothing adds the most.
The relation to a heat-equation picture is not a loose analogy; it is made exact in the Black-Scholes treatment
later in the course.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Buying an option is not the same as buying the underlying.** The option has a limited life, a premium that
  decays, and a payoff that is zero in a large part of the outcomes. Being right about direction is not enough;
  the move has to be large enough and early enough to beat the premium.
- **An option that expires out of the money is not a mistake in the contract.** It is the outcome the buyer
  paid to avoid being forced into. The premium is the cost of the insurance, whether or not the claim is made.
- **A call is not a bullish bet and a put is not a bearish one by definition.** A put held against a share is
  protection, and a written put is a bet that the price will not fall far. What the position is depends on the
  other holdings and on which side of the contract you are on.
- **American and European do not describe where the option trades.** They describe when it can be exercised.
