## IN ONE SENTENCE

A swap is an agreement to trade one stream of payments for another over several dates, so that each side ends up
with the cash-flow shape it actually wants.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Borrowers and lenders rarely want the same kind of interest. A company with a floating-rate loan may prefer to
know its costs in advance; a pension fund holding long fixed-rate bonds may fear that rates will rise. Each could
rearrange its balance sheet by selling and buying the underlying securities, but that is slow, expensive, and
changes the principal as well as the interest.

A swap changes only the stream of payments. Nobody lends or borrows anything new, and the principal stays where it
is. This is why the market is so large: it is the cheapest way to change the *shape* of an exposure without
touching the asset or liability that produced it.

## DEFINITION

In an **interest rate swap**, one side pays interest at a rate fixed at the start and the other pays interest at a
rate that resets periodically against a reference index. Both are computed on the same **notional**, an amount
that is only a unit of account and never changes hands.

The side paying the agreed rate is the **fixed leg**; the side paying the reset rate is the **floating leg**. On
each payment date only the difference is transferred. The rate on the fixed leg is chosen so that the swap has
zero value at inception: no one pays the other to enter it. That rate is the swap rate.

The same idea reaches beyond interest rates, and the rest of the family is defined by what the legs pay:

- A **CDS** pays one side if a named borrower defaults, in exchange for a periodic fee from the other.
- A **total return swap** pays one side everything an asset earns, income and price change, in exchange for a
  floating rate from the other. It gives the economics of owning the asset without owning it.
- A **basis** swap exchanges two floating rates against each other, for example two different reference indices
  or two currencies. The **basis** is the spread that makes the exchange fair.

An illustrative example, with numbers chosen for ease rather than taken from any market. Take a notional of
100 million and annual payments. One side pays a fixed 3% and receives the floating rate. If the floating rate
resets to 4% for a period, the fixed payer receives net 1% of the notional, 1 million. If it resets to 2%, the
fixed payer pays net 1 million. The fixed payer has in effect turned a floating-rate cost into a fixed one.

## FORMALLY

Let the payment dates be $t_1, \dots, t_n$ with accrual fractions $\delta_i$, and let $P(0, t_i)$ be today's price
of one unit received at $t_i$. At a floating reset, the floating leg pays the forward rate $F_i$ for that period.
The value to the fixed-rate payer, per unit notional, is

$$
V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta_i \, P(0, t_i)\,(F_i - K),
$$

where $K$ is the fixed rate. The swap is therefore a portfolio of $n$ forward contracts on the short rate, each
struck at the same $K$. The swap rate $K^*$ is the value of $K$ that sets $V = 0$:

$$
K^* = \frac{\sum_i \delta_i \, P(0, t_i)\, F_i}{\sum_i \delta_i \, P(0, t_i)}.
$$

It is a weighted average of the forward rates, weighted by the discount factors. After inception the value moves
away from zero as the forward rates move, and it is carried almost entirely by the fixed leg: the floating leg
resets to market at each date, so its value stays close to its notional and its duration is short.

Two things follow. First, the exposure is measured by $V$ and by its sensitivity to rates, not by the notional.
Second, the swap rate is not an independent quantity: given the discount curve, it is fixed by arbitrage, since
the same payments can be assembled from the forwards.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Conserved total, redistributed in time">
A swap moves nothing into or out of the system on net: what one side pays, the other receives, and at inception
the total value exchanged is zero. What it changes is how the same stock of risk is distributed over time and
over holders, in the way a conserved quantity can be redistributed without being created.

This is also why the notional is the wrong measure of size. It plays the role of a unit of normalisation, like
the area through which a flux is measured, and the quantity that matters is the flux, here the net payment and
its sensitivity to the rate.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Notional is not exposure and not money at risk.** A swap on 100 million does not put 100 million at risk.
  The headline total of derivatives outstanding adds up notionals, and for that reason overstates risk by a very
  large factor.
- **The fixed payer is not simply betting that rates rise.** The fixed payer gains when floating rates end up above
  the agreed rate, but is often a borrower using the swap to remove an exposure rather than create one.
- **A swap is not a loan.** No principal is advanced. The only credit exposure is to the other side's ability to
  make the net payments, which is why collateral and clearing exist.
- **The swap rate is not a government yield.** The two differ by a spread that reflects credit, funding and
  dealer balance-sheet conditions, and that is the object in the figure above.
