## IN ONE SENTENCE

Gross domestic product is the value of what an economy produces in a period, counted so that it equals what is spent on that production and what is earned from it.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Before the 1930s there was no agreed figure for the size of an economy or for whether it was growing. Governments had data on tax receipts, trade and some prices, and policymakers in the Depression had to act with no common measure of how far output had fallen. Simon Kuznets built the first systematic national income accounts for the United States in the early 1930s, and the framework was extended and standardised during the Second World War, when governments needed to know how much of total output could be diverted to the war effort without the civilian economy collapsing.

The result is a bookkeeping system, not a physical measurement. Every sale is also a purchase, and every purchase is someone's income, so the same total can be counted three ways: as output, as spending, or as income. GDP is the number the accounts agree on, and nearly all macroeconomic discussion, from central bank decisions to debt ratios, is expressed relative to it.

## DEFINITION

**Nominal GDP** is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a period, at the prices of that period. "Final" means that goods used up in making other goods are not counted again; only the value added at each stage enters.

**Real GDP** is the same quantity valued at the prices of a fixed base period, so that it changes only when quantities change. The ratio of the two is the **GDP deflator**, a price index for everything produced. If nominal GDP grows by 5% in a year and the deflator rises by 2%, real GDP has grown by about 3%. Growth, in the ordinary sense, means real growth.

The **expenditure identity** says that output equals the spending on it: consumption by households, investment by firms (including new housing and changes in inventories), government purchases of goods and services, and exports minus imports. It is an identity, true by construction. Its use is to decompose a change in output into the categories that drove it and to see the accounting links between sectors.

GDP is a **flow**: it is output per unit of time, quoted per quarter or per year. It is not a **stock**. The capital of a country, its debts and its wealth are stocks, built up by past flows. A country with a large GDP can be poor in wealth and one with small output can hold large assets, and comparing a stock with a flow without a time period attached is a category error.

Several things that matter to people are outside the count by construction. Unpaid household work, leisure, the depletion of natural resources and the distribution of income between people do not enter, and neither does the purchase of an existing asset: buying a house built last year, or a share of a company, is a transfer, while the fee paid to the agent is output.

## FORMALLY

Let $Y$ be output, $C$ consumption, $I$ investment, $G$ government purchases, $X$ exports and $M$ imports. Then

$$
Y = C + I + G + (X - M).
$$

With nominal GDP $Y_t$, real GDP $y_t$ and deflator $P_t$ normalised to $1$ in the base period,

$$
P_t = \frac{Y_t}{y_t}, \qquad g_{Y} \approx g_{y} + \pi,
$$

where $g_Y$ and $g_y$ are the growth rates of nominal and real GDP and $\pi$ is the growth of the deflator; the approximation is accurate when the rates are small. The contribution of a component to growth is its growth rate weighted by its share of the total,

$$
g_y \approx \frac{C}{Y}\,g_C + \frac{I}{Y}\,g_I + \frac{G}{Y}\,g_G + \frac{X-M}{Y}\,g_{X-M},
$$

which is why a small component can dominate a recession: a share of one fifth falling by a tenth subtracts as much from growth as a share of two thirds falling by a thirtieth.

Rearranging the identity in terms of saving shows the link between sectors: the balance on trade, $X - M$, equals private saving minus investment plus the government surplus. A country that spends more than it produces must be financed by someone, which is where capital flows and exchange rates enter.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Flows and stocks, and conservation as bookkeeping">
The distinction between a flow and a stock is the one between a current and a charge, or between a rate and the quantity it accumulates into: the stock is the integral of the net flow, and a balance sheet at a date is a sum over history. The expenditure identity is a conservation statement of an accounting kind, in that every unit counted as output appears once as spending and once as income.

The analogy breaks at what is being conserved. Charge is a physical quantity with a definition independent of the observer. Real GDP depends on the choice of base-period prices and of how quality change is valued, so it is a constructed index whose revisions and conventions are part of the measurement, closer to a coarse-grained observable defined by a procedure than to a conserved density.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **GDP is not wealth.** It is a flow of output per period, and wealth is a stock. A country can have high GDP and heavy debts, or low GDP and large reserves.
- **GDP is not welfare.** It counts the value of what is produced at market prices and so leaves out unpaid work, leisure, environmental damage and how income is shared. A disaster followed by repairs raises it.
- **Nominal growth is not real growth.** A rise in nominal GDP can be entirely a rise in prices. Real GDP removes this, but only as well as the deflator measures prices.
- **The identity is not a causal model.** $Y = C + I + G + (X-M)$ holds always, and so it cannot say that raising $G$ raises $Y$. Whether it does depends on what happens to the other terms, and that is a question for a model and for data.
- **The figure is an estimate that is revised.** The first release of quarterly GDP is based on incomplete data and is revised, sometimes substantially, over the following years, so the number a market reacts to is not the number the history books end up with.
