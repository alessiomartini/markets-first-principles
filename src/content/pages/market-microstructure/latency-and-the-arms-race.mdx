## IN ONE SENTENCE

Because a **matching engine** processes messages one at a time in the order they arrive, being first is worth money,
and firms will spend up to that amount to be first.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Nobody designed markets to reward speed. It falls out of two design decisions that each look reasonable alone.

The first is serial matching: the engine processes messages sequentially, so there is always a first message and a
last one. The second is **price-time priority**, which resolves ties by arrival order. Together they make the
sequence of arrivals the market's ground truth, and turn every stale quote into a prize awarded to whoever reaches
it first.

Once that is true, speed is not a strategy but an input, like electricity. Firms invest in it until the marginal
cost of another microsecond equals the marginal revenue from winning slightly more races — which is why the arms
race has a natural equilibrium in spending, and why that equilibrium can consume a large fraction of the profits
the market was supposed to distribute.

## DEFINITION

**Colocation** is renting rack space in the exchange's own building so the cable to the **matching engine** is
metres rather than kilometres. Exchanges sell it openly and equalise cable lengths inside the facility, which makes
it an expensive tier rather than a hidden advantage.

**Matching latency** is the time between an order arriving and the response leaving. What matters competitively is
the tail rather than the mean: a system that is fast on average and occasionally slow loses exactly the races worth
winning, because those are the moments when everyone is trying at once.

A **race condition** is the situation these produce. A price becomes stale, several participants simultaneously try
to act — some to take the quote, the maker to cancel it — and only one message can arrive first. **High-frequency
trading** is the set of strategies whose edge depends on winning them. It is at least two businesses with opposite
social valences: electronic **market maker**s narrowing spreads, and latency arbitrageurs picking off stale quotes.

**Maker-taker pricing** shapes the incentives, paying a rebate to resting orders and charging takers. It also
distorts what the displayed price means, since the effective price differs from the quoted one by the fee.

## FORMALLY

The value of speed is the value of winning races, and it can be written down. If a stale quote is worth $\delta$
when it can be picked off, and $n$ firms compete to reach it with latencies $\ell_i$, the prize goes to
$\arg\min_i \ell_i$ and the expected revenue to firm $i$ is

$$
R_i = \delta \cdot \lambda \cdot \Pr\left[\ell_i < \min_{j \ne i} \ell_j\right]
$$

with $\lambda$ the rate at which such opportunities appear. Firms invest in latency until marginal cost equals
marginal revenue, and in the symmetric equilibrium the total spend approaches $\delta \lambda$ — the entire value of
the mispricings. The prize is competed away into infrastructure rather than into narrower spreads, which is the
formal statement of the objection to the arms race and the argument for batch auctions that replace the continuous
race with a discrete one.

The empirical counterpart is the distribution of inter-arrival times. If updates were independent events at a
constant rate, gaps would be exponential with a well-defined mean. They are not: the distribution is heavy-tailed
and spans many orders of magnitude, because activity clusters. Trades trigger quote updates which trigger further
trades.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="No characteristic time scale, and self-excitation">
The absence of an exponential inter-arrival distribution is the important observation, because it means market time
has no natural unit. There is no correct sampling frequency: choose milliseconds and most bins are empty, choose
minutes and you average over bursts that contain everything interesting. This is why microstructure works in *event
time* — indexing by trade or update number rather than by the clock — which is a change of variable that restores
approximate stationarity.

The clustering itself is well described by a self-exciting point process, where each event temporarily raises the
intensity of further events:

$$
\lambda(t) = \lambda_0 + \sum_{t_i < t} \phi(t - t_i)
$$

This is the Hawkes process, and it is the same mathematics as earthquake aftershock sequences, with $\phi$ playing
the role of the Omori kernel. The branching ratio — the expected number of events directly triggered by one event —
is measured in real markets at values uncomfortably close to one, meaning markets sit near the critical point where
activity becomes self-sustaining. That proximity to criticality is one candidate explanation for flash crashes, and
it connects this page directly to the volatility clustering of Track 3.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **HFT is not one thing.** Market making and latency arbitrage have opposite effects on the cost of trading, and
  most public argument conflates them.
- **Speed is not an edge, it is a cost of entry.** Everyone competitive is fast. What differs is who is fastest at
  the moments that matter, which is a tail property.
- **Colocation is not privileged access.** It is sold to anyone and cable lengths are equalised. The advantage it
  confers is real and is priced accordingly.
- **Faster is not automatically worse for investors.** Spreads narrowed substantially as speed increased. The
  honest objection is about resources burned in races, not about spreads.
