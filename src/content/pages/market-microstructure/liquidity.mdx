## IN ONE SENTENCE

Liquidity is how cheaply you can change your mind, and it is three separate quantities that people insist on calling
by one name.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Liquidity is not a property of an asset but a service, supplied by participants who are willing to take the other
side of your trade before they have found someone to pass it on to. Somebody has to warehouse the position in
between, and warehousing costs money and carries risk.

This is why liquidity is never free and never guaranteed. It is priced, it is supplied by firms making a commercial
decision, and it is withdrawn exactly when warehousing is most dangerous — which is to say, in a crisis. Anyone who
models liquidity as a fixed transaction cost has assumed away the thing that makes it interesting: it is a
behaviour, not a constant, and it disappears in a way that is correlated across every asset at once.

## DEFINITION

Three distinct measurements share the name, and they routinely disagree.

The **spread** — the gap between best **bid** and best **ask** — is the round-trip cost of trading a small
quantity immediately. It is the most quoted measure and the least relevant to anyone trading size.

**Depth** is the quantity resting at each price level, or cumulatively out to some distance from the mid. It
determines what a large order actually pays, and a tight spread with no depth behind it is a market that looks
cheap and is not.

**Resilience** is how quickly the **order book** refills after being consumed. It decides whether an order can be
worked patiently over time or must pay its impact at once, and it is the least measured of the three.

Two volume-based measures complete the picture. **Average daily volume** is the unit in which order size is
universally discussed, because a trade's cost depends on its ratio to ADV rather than on its absolute size.
**Turnover** normalises volume by the amount outstanding, which is how activity is compared across assets of very
different sizes.

Behind all of them sits **latent liquidity**: the interest that exists but is not displayed. The visible book is a
small fraction of true supply and demand, which is why it predicts poorly what would trade at a given price.

## FORMALLY

The three measurements can be read off one object, the response of execution cost to size. For small $Q$ the cost
per unit is set by the spread alone,

$$
C(Q) \approx \frac{s_t}{2}, \qquad Q \to 0
$$

For larger $Q$ it is set by the shape of the book, and empirically by the square-root law once the book's reaction
is included,

$$
C(Q) \approx Y \sigma \sqrt{\frac{Q}{V}}, \qquad Q \gtrsim \text{top-of-book depth}
$$

with $V$ the **average daily volume** and $Y$ a constant of order one. Resilience does not appear in either
expression, because it is not a property of a single trade — it governs how $C$ behaves when the same quantity is
split across time, and therefore whether the second expression can be beaten by patience.

The practical consequence is that no single number summarises liquidity. A market is characterised by a *curve*,
and quoting the spread is reporting one point on it — the point corresponding to a trade size nobody with a real
position cares about.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A transport coefficient, not a state variable">
Liquidity behaves like a transport coefficient: it is defined only relative to a perturbation of a given size and
frequency, and quoting it without those is meaningless in the same way as quoting a conductivity without saying at
what field strength.

The analogy earns its place by predicting the failure mode. Transport coefficients are properties of a system near
equilibrium, and they stop describing anything when the system is driven hard. That is precisely what happens in a
crisis: the linear-response picture in which trading costs a predictable spread breaks down, liquidity providers
withdraw, and the market moves into a regime where the coefficient is not merely different but undefined. Risk
models that treat liquidity as a fixed haircut are extrapolating a near-equilibrium quantity into exactly the
regime where it does not exist.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Volume is not liquidity.** They diverge most in a crisis, when volume explodes and depth collapses. High
  **turnover** with no depth is churn.
- **The spread is one point on a curve.** For any institutional size it is close to irrelevant, and improvements in
  quoted spreads over the last two decades have not been matched by improvements in depth.
- **Liquidity is not a property of the asset.** It is a property of the market for the asset at a moment, supplied
  by firms who can stop supplying it.
- **The visible book is not the supply curve.** Most interest is latent, which cuts both ways: there is more real
  liquidity than the book shows, and less of it is committed.
