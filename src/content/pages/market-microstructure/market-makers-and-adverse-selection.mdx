## IN ONE SENTENCE

A market maker sells immediacy at a price, and that price is set almost entirely by how often the buyer of
immediacy turns out to know more than the seller.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Someone has to be willing to trade when you want to, rather than when a natural counterparty happens to appear.
That service — immediacy — is what a **market maker** sells, and the **bid-ask spread** is its price.

If all incoming orders were uninformed, the business would be close to free money: quote both sides, capture the
spread thousands of times a day, end flat. It is not, because some traders hit your quote precisely because they
expect the price to move. You buy from them just before the price falls. That asymmetry, not operating cost, is what
sets the spread, and understanding it explains nearly every feature of modern electronic markets — the microsecond
quote lifetimes, the cancellation rates above 95%, and the entire economics of retail order routing.

## DEFINITION

**Quoting** is continuously posting a **bid** and an **ask** at which you will trade. It is the act that supplies
**liquidity**.

**Inventory risk** is the exposure a maker accumulates from doing so. Buy from three sellers in a row and you are
long whether you wanted to be or not, so quotes are skewed rather than symmetric around fair value — leaning in
whichever direction unloads the position.

**Adverse selection** is the deeper problem: the counterparties most eager to trade against your quote are
disproportionately the ones who know something. **Toxic flow** is the name for order flow that systematically
predicts the next move. The term is descriptive rather than moral — a fund executing well-researched positions
produces toxic flow simply by being right.

Two market-structure features follow directly. **Payment for order flow** is a wholesaler paying a retail broker to
route uninformed **retail flow** for **internalisation**, which is profitable precisely because that flow is not
toxic. **Tick size** sets a floor on the spread, and where the floor binds, competition shifts from price to
**queue priority** — makers can no longer undercut, so they race to be earlier in the line instead.

## FORMALLY

Three spreads must be distinguished, and their differences are the whole story. For a trade at price $p_t$ with
sign $\varepsilon_t$ and mid $m_t$, the *effective* half-spread is what the taker actually paid,

$$
s^{\text{eff}}_t = \varepsilon_t \left( p_t - m_t \right)
$$

and the *realised* half-spread is what the maker kept, measured after the price has had time to move,

$$
s^{\text{real}}_t = \varepsilon_t \left( p_t - m_{t + \Delta} \right)
$$

Their difference is the adverse-selection component, also called price impact of the trade:

$$
s^{\text{eff}}_t - s^{\text{real}}_t = \varepsilon_t \left( m_{t+\Delta} - m_t \right)
$$

The interpretation is exact and worth pausing on. The taker pays the effective spread. The maker keeps only the
realised spread. The difference is handed back to the market through the price drifting in the direction of the
trade — which is to say, the maker's gross margin is reduced by exactly the information content of the flow it
absorbed. Where the flow is uninformed the two spreads coincide; where it is toxic the realised spread can be
negative, and the maker loses money on every trade at a spread that looked profitable when quoted.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A measurement that is not independent of what it measures">
The maker's difficulty is not noise but correlation between the noise and the state. If orders arrived
independently of future price moves, quoting would be a simple inventory-control problem: a random walk with a
restoring force, solvable and profitable. The distribution of arrivals is instead conditioned on the very quantity
the maker is trying to estimate.

The closest honest analogy is a detector whose trigger rate depends on the signal it is trying to measure — every
event you record is preferentially one that will make you wrong, and averaging over your own sample does not
converge to the truth. The maker's defences are the ones a physicist would reach for: shorten the integration time
so stale quotes cannot be picked off, and classify events by source so that the biased population can be priced
separately. That second defence is exactly what **payment for order flow** industrialises, which is why the
argument about it is an argument about who gets to trade against the unbiased sample.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Market makers are not obliged to quote.** Formal obligations exist on some venues and are weak. Quotes widen
  or vanish under stress, which is when the obligation would have mattered.
- **The spread is not a fee.** It is compensation for adverse selection, which is why it widens around
  announcements and in illiquid names rather than tracking operating cost.
- **High cancellation rates are not manipulation.** A resting quote is an option the maker has written and is
  constantly repricing. Above 95% cancellation is normal and mechanical.
- **Toxic flow is not bad behaviour.** It is informed trading, which is the mechanism by which prices become
  correct. A market with no toxic flow would have no price discovery.
