## IN ONE SENTENCE

An order is not just a price and a quantity; it is a set of instructions about what to do when the market does not
cooperate, and those instructions are where the cost lives.

## WHY IT EXISTS

A **market order** and a **limit order** are the two pure strategies, and both are unusable at size. A market order
for a large quantity walks the book and pays a terrible average price. A limit order for the same quantity sits
visible in the book, announcing your intention to everyone, and gets filled only when someone is willing to take the
other side — which is disproportionately when they know something.

Every order type in this page exists to occupy the space between those two failures. They are, without exception,
mechanisms for controlling *how much you reveal* and *how long you wait*, because those are the only two dials
available. Execution is the engineering discipline of turning a decision to trade into a position without giving
away what you are doing.

## DEFINITION

Two instructions govern what happens to the unexecuted remainder. **IOC** — immediate-or-cancel — executes whatever
can be filled right now and cancels the rest, leaving no standing order for anyone to observe. **FOK** —
fill-or-kill — demands the whole quantity at once or nothing, which is the right choice only when a **partial fill**
is worse than no trade at all, such as one leg of a two-leg arbitrage.

A **fill** is an executed trade against your order; a large order typically generates many fills at successive
prices, and their average is your cost. An **iceberg order** displays only a slice of its true size and replenishes
it, buying concealment at the price of **queue priority**, since the hidden remainder usually loses its place in
line each time the slice refreshes.

Above these sit scheduling algorithms, which slice a parent order over time. **TWAP** spreads it evenly across the
period. **VWAP** spreads it in proportion to expected volume, which is the natural improvement, and is also the
benchmark most institutional execution is measured against.

**Slippage** is the difference between the price you expected when you decided and the price you achieved. It
bundles the **bid-ask spread**, your own **market impact**, and the market's drift while you worked the order —
and separating those three is the entire discipline of execution analysis.

## FORMALLY

Walking the book is a deterministic calculation once the book is known. With ask levels at prices $a_1 < a_2 <
\dots$ holding quantities $q_1, q_2, \dots$, a **market order** of size $Q$ consumes levels until they are
exhausted, so the average execution price is

$$
\bar{p}(Q) = \frac{1}{Q} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \, q_i, \qquad
k = \min \left\lbrace n : \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i \ge Q \right\rbrace
$$

and the cost relative to the mid is $\bar{p}(Q) - m_t$. This is the *mechanical* cost, computable exactly from a
snapshot, and it is what the figure below plots.

It is also an underestimate of the true cost, and understanding why is the point of the page. The calculation
assumes the book stands still while you consume it. In reality, taking liquidity causes the remaining quotes to be
pulled and repriced, so a real order of size $Q$ costs more than walking a static book predicts. The gap between the
two is **market impact**, and it is why the empirical cost grows as $\sqrt{Q}$ rather than following the snapshot
curve.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Adiabatic versus sudden">
The distinction between the two costs above is exactly the distinction between a sudden and an adiabatic
perturbation. Dump the whole order at once and the system has no time to respond: you sample the instantaneous
configuration of the book and pay the mechanical cost, plus whatever the survivors charge on the way out.

Work the order slowly and the system relaxes between slices — cancelled quotes are replaced, the **latent
liquidity** behind the visible book has time to surface, and the price partially reverts because most impact is
**transient impact**. Trading slowly is therefore an adiabatic process and costs less per unit, with the usual
catch: the slower you go, the longer you are exposed to the market moving for reasons unrelated to you. That
trade-off between impact and timing risk is the whole content of optimal execution, and Almgren–Chriss is its
harmonic-oscillator-level solution.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **A limit order is not a safer market order.** It removes price risk and adds execution risk, and the two are
  correlated in the worst way: the times it fills are the times you would rather it had not.
- **VWAP is a benchmark, not a goal.** A broker measured against VWAP tracks the market by construction, and will
  buy into a rising price because the benchmark rises with it. Implementation shortfall is the honest measure.
- **Icebergs are not free concealment.** The hidden portion generally forfeits queue position on each refresh, so
  it trades meaningfully less often than displayed size.
- **Slippage is not one number.** Spread, impact and drift have different causes and different remedies, and a
  report that does not separate them cannot be acted on.
