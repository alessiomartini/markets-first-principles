## IN ONE SENTENCE

Prices move because orders arrive, not because value changed, and the amount they move grows as the square root of
how much you trade.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The textbook account has prices moving when news changes an asset's fundamental value. Look at the tape and that
account collapses immediately: the overwhelming majority of price changes happen with no identifiable news, and the
timing of moves matches the arrival of orders far better than the arrival of information.

The mechanical account is better. A price changes when someone consumes the resting liquidity at the touch, and
that happens when an order arrives. Information matters, but it matters *through* order flow — a trader who knows
something must trade to express it, and it is the trade, not the knowledge, that moves the price.

This reframing is what makes impact the central quantity of Track 2. It is simultaneously the largest cost any
strategy at size pays, the mechanism by which information enters prices, and the constraint that caps how much
capital any strategy can absorb.

## DEFINITION

**Order flow** is the stream of arriving buy and sell orders, signed by which side initiated. Its most important
empirical property is that it is strongly *autocorrelated* — persisting for hours or days — because large orders are
split into many small ones by exactly the execution algorithms of the previous page.

**Order-flow imbalance** is net buyer-initiated minus seller-initiated volume over an interval. Over short horizons
it explains most of the variance in returns, which is less useful than it sounds: you would have to know it before
it happened.

**Market impact** is the extent to which your own trading moves the price against you. It splits into two pieces
whose separation is genuinely contested. **Permanent impact** is the part that does not decay — conventionally read
as the information the market inferred from your trade. **Transient impact** is the part that relaxes as the book
refills, and it is what makes patient execution worthwhile.

The **propagator** $G(\tau)$ is the function describing how much of a single trade's effect survives $\tau$ events
later. It is the object that reconciles the two facts that otherwise cannot both be true.

## FORMALLY

The empirical law is remarkably robust across markets, decades and asset classes. A metaorder of total size $Q$
executed over a day moves the price by

$$
\mathcal{I}(Q) \;=\; Y \, \sigma \sqrt{\frac{Q}{V}}
$$

with $\sigma$ the daily volatility, $V$ the daily volume, and $Y$ a dimensionless constant close to one. Note what
this expression does *not* contain: any parameter describing the asset, the venue or the era. It is close to a
dimensional-analysis result, and its exponent of one half is measured, not assumed.

The propagator resolves the puzzle. Write the price as past **order flow** convolved with a memory kernel,

$$
p_t \;=\; \sum_{t' < t} G(t - t') \, \varepsilon_{t'} \sqrt{v_{t'}} \;+\; \text{noise}
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the trade sign. Order flow is persistent, with autocorrelation decaying as $\tau^{-\gamma}$
for small $\gamma$. If $G$ were constant, that persistence would make prices trivially predictable and the market
trivially arbitrageable. It is not, because $G$ decays as

$$
G(\tau) \sim \tau^{-\beta}, \qquad \beta \approx \frac{1 - \gamma}{2}
$$

and that relation between the two exponents is not a coincidence. It is the condition that makes the predictable
part of the price cancel exactly — a no-arbitrage constraint expressed as a relation between two independently
measurable exponents.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Linear response with a memory kernel">
The propagator equation is a linear response function in the strict sense: the observable (price) is the
convolution of the perturbation (order flow) with a kernel encoding the system's memory. Everything a physicist
knows about response functions transfers directly — causality constrains $G$ to vanish for negative argument, the
kernel's decay sets the relaxation spectrum, and the response to correlated forcing is not the response to white
noise.

The genuinely striking result is the exponent relation $\beta \approx (1-\gamma)/2$. Two quantities measured by
completely different means — the persistence of order flow, and the decay of price response — are locked together
by the requirement that the market not be trivially predictable. This is a fluctuation-dissipation relation in all
but name: a constraint tying the system's response to the statistics of its own fluctuations, enforced here by
arbitrage rather than by thermodynamics.

The square-root law is the same physics integrated up. Its concavity is not a fitted curvature but what you get
when a persistent flow is convolved with a decaying kernel, and the fact that $Y$ carries no asset-specific
parameters is the strongest hint available that the mechanism is structural rather than behavioural.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Impact is not the spread.** The spread is what you pay to cross once; impact is what your own trading does to
  the price, and at size it is much larger.
- **Concave does not mean cheap.** Cost per unit falls with size, but total cost still rises. Doubling size costs
  about 1.4 times as much, not half as much.
- **Correlation between flow and returns is not a strategy.** Imbalance explains returns contemporaneously; to
  profit you would need to forecast the imbalance, which is a different and much harder problem.
- **"Permanent" is a modelling choice, not an observation.** With any finite sample, permanent impact is
  indistinguishable from a slowly decaying transient one. This is one of the places where the field genuinely does
  not know the answer.
