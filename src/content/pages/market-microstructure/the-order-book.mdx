## IN ONE SENTENCE

The order book is the standing list of every price at which someone has publicly committed to trade, and the price
you see quoted is just its top edge.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Buyers and sellers do not arrive at the same time. Someone who wants to buy at two in the afternoon needs a seller
who happens to want to sell at two in the afternoon, and in a market of any size that coincidence is rare enough
that waiting for it would be intolerable.

The order book solves this by letting participants leave their intentions behind. A trader who is willing to sell at
a price but is in no hurry writes the offer down and goes away; a trader who arrives later and *is* in a hurry
executes against it immediately. The book converts a matching problem across time into a matching problem at a
point in time, and the price of that convenience — paid by the impatient side to the patient side — is the spread.
Everything else in Track&nbsp;2 is a consequence of this trade between patience and immediacy.

## DEFINITION

The **order book** is the complete set of **limit order**s currently resting at a venue, organised by price and,
within each price, by time of arrival. A limit order states a price and a quantity and waits; it supplies liquidity.
A **market order** states only a quantity and executes immediately against whatever is resting; it removes
liquidity.

The highest resting buy price is the **bid**, the lowest resting sell price is the **ask**, and the gap between them
is the **bid-ask spread**. Together they are *the touch*. The quantity resting at each level is the **depth**, and
cumulative depth is what determines the price a large order will actually achieve — which is why the spread alone
tells you almost nothing about the cost of a serious trade.

Matching is performed by the **matching engine**, a deliberately serial machine that processes incoming messages one
at a time in the order it receives them. Nearly all equity venues match on **price-time priority**: best price
first, and among equal prices, earliest arrival first. Your **queue priority** — your place in that line — therefore
determines not only whether you trade but *when*, and orders at the back of a queue tend to fill precisely when the
level is about to be exhausted and the price is about to move through them.

## FORMALLY

Write the book as a signed density of resting volume over price. At time $t$, let $\rho_b(p, t)$ be the volume
resting on the bid side at price $p$, and $\rho_a(p, t)$ the volume on the ask side. The best quotes are the
supports' edges:

$$
b_t = \max \left\lbrace p : \rho_b(p,t) > 0 \right\rbrace, \qquad
a_t = \min \left\lbrace p : \rho_a(p,t) > 0 \right\rbrace
$$

with mid price $m_t = (a_t + b_t)/2$ and spread $s_t = a_t - b_t$. Cumulative depth out to a distance $\delta$ from
the mid is the integral of the density,

$$
Q_b(\delta, t) = \int_{m_t - \delta}^{m_t} \rho_b(p, t)\, dp
$$

and it is $Q_b$, not $s_t$, that sets the cost of a large trade. A **market order** of size $Q$ walks the book until
the cumulative depth it has consumed equals $Q$, so its average execution price is worse than $a_t$ by an amount
determined entirely by the shape of $\rho_a$ near the touch.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Reaction–diffusion with annihilation at an interface">
The book is two populations of particles — resting buy volume and resting sell volume — occupying opposite sides of
a moving boundary. Orders are deposited at a rate that depends on distance from the mid, they are removed by
cancellation at some rate, and they annihilate pairwise on contact when an incoming market order crosses the spread.
That is a reaction–diffusion system, $A + B \to \emptyset$, with the mid price as the interface between the two
phases.

The framing is not decorative. It predicts the shape of the average book: because annihilation is fastest where the
two populations meet, density is suppressed near the interface and grows away from it, which is exactly the
V-shaped profile seen in the figure below. It also predicts that the price moves diffusively even when order flow is
strongly correlated, because the boundary's motion is governed by the *local* imbalance rather than the total. The
latent order book models of Track&nbsp;2's later pages take this literally and get the square-root impact law out of
it.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **The spread is not the cost of trading.** It is the cost of trading *the first share*. For any size worth caring
  about, the cost is set by cumulative depth, and depth is far more variable than spread.
- **Most orders never trade.** Cancellation rates above 95% are normal and are not evidence of manipulation; a
  resting order is an option the market maker is constantly repricing.
- **The book is not demand.** It shows the interest people are willing to *display*. Large participants
  systematically hide theirs, which is why the visible book is a poor estimate of what would actually trade — the
  latent book is much larger and much less impressed by your order.
- **A deep book is not a stable one.** Depth is quoted, not committed: it can be cancelled in microseconds and
  disappears fastest exactly when it is most wanted.
