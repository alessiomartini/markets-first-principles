## IN ONE SENTENCE

An option can be copied by trading the underlying continuously, so its price is the cost of that copy, and the only
thing about the future that the cost depends on is how much the price will wiggle.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The previous page showed that if a payoff can be replicated, its price is the cost of the replication. For a stock
option that left a practical gap: how many shares, held when, and what does the answer depend on? Earlier attempts
priced options by guessing the stock's expected growth and a risk premium, and the guesses were part of the answer.

The contribution of Bachelier (1900) and of Black, Scholes and Merton (1973) was to give the copy explicitly. In
their setting the expected growth of the stock drops out, and what remains is one number describing the size of the
random moves, the volatility. That is why the formula became the common language of option markets: traders do not
agree on where the stock is going, but they can agree on a volatility, and quote prices in that unit.

## DEFINITION

An option's value changes when the underlying moves, when time passes and when the market's view of volatility
changes. The standard sensitivities, called the Greeks, separate these.

**Delta** is the change in the option's value per unit change in the underlying. A call has a delta between 0 and
1, close to 0 far out of the money and close to 1 deep in the money. **Gamma** is the change in delta per unit move
of the underlying, so it measures how quickly a hedge goes stale. **Vega** is the change in value per unit change
in volatility. **Theta** is the change in value as time passes with nothing else moving, and for a bought option it
is usually negative: the option loses time value each day.

**Delta hedging** means holding the opposite of the option's delta in the underlying, so that small moves in the
underlying cancel to first order. It has to be repeated as delta changes, and what it leaves behind is mostly gamma:
a hedger who is long gamma profits from large moves in either direction and pays for it through theta, and a hedger
who is short gamma does the reverse.

The **Black-Scholes formula** is the price of a European option that follows from continuous delta hedging when the
underlying has constant volatility and the riskless rate is known. Of its inputs, every one is observed except
volatility. Turning the formula around, the volatility that reproduces a quoted option price is the **implied
volatility**. It is a way of expressing a price, not a measurement of the future: a quote of 20% means that the
option costs what the formula gives at 20%.

## FORMALLY

Let the underlying follow geometric Brownian motion, $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$, and let cash earn the
constant rate $r$. For an option worth $V(S,t)$, hold $-\partial V/\partial S$ shares against one option. Over a
short time the random term cancels, the portfolio is riskless, and it must earn $r$. This gives the
Black-Scholes equation

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0 ,
$$

in which $\mu$ does not appear. In terms of the Greeks, with $\Theta=\partial V/\partial t$, $\Delta=\partial V/\partial S$ and
$\Gamma=\partial^2 V/\partial S^2$, this reads $\Theta + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2\Gamma + rS\Delta - rV = 0$. For a call with strike $K$
and expiry $T$ the solution is

$$
C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad
d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T},
$$

where $N$ is the standard normal distribution function. The delta of the call is $N(d_1)$.

Bachelier's earlier model has the underlying move by additive rather than proportional steps, $dF = \sigma_n\,dW$,
for a forward price $F$. With $d = (F-K)/(\sigma_n\sqrt{T})$ the undiscounted call value is

$$
(F-K)\,N(d) + \sigma_n \sqrt{T}\,\varphi(d),
$$

with $\varphi$ the standard normal density. Here $\sigma_n$ is in price units rather than percent. The model allows
negative prices, which was a defect for stocks and became a convenience for interest rates and for commodities at
times when prices went below zero, and many rate options are now quoted in this normal volatility.

What is left after discrete hedging can be read from the same relation. Hedge at the volatility $\sigma_i$ used for
the price, and let the underlying move with realised volatility $\sigma_r$. Over a small interval the hedged
portfolio gains approximately

$$
\tfrac12 \Gamma S^2 \left( \sigma_r^2 - \sigma_i^2 \right) dt ,
$$

so the total is the sum of this over the option's life, weighted by where the gamma was. If realised variance
exceeds implied variance, a long option position wins, and conversely. The bet is on volatility, not on direction.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="The diffusion equation and the heat kernel">
With a change of variables, the Black-Scholes equation becomes the heat equation, $\partial_\tau u = \tfrac12\sigma^2
\partial_x^2 u$, and the formula is the heat kernel, the Gaussian solution of a diffusion started from a point, applied
to the payoff. Bachelier's model is that diffusion directly, written five years before Einstein's paper on Brownian
motion. Volatility plays the role of a diffusion constant, and time to expiry is the time over which the spreading
happens.

The analogy fails where it matters in markets. A diffusion constant is a property of the medium and does not change
with the observer, whereas volatility is not constant: it clusters, jumps, and differs by strike, and the price of
risk in the real world is what the formula was constructed to avoid, not to explain.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **The formula does not forecast.** It converts a volatility into a price, and a price back into a volatility. The
  expected return of the stock is absent because the hedge removes it, not because it is zero.
- **Implied volatility is not expected volatility.** It includes the price of the protection, so on average it sits
  above what is later realised in many markets; reading it as a forecast confuses a quote with an expectation.
- **A delta hedge is not a perfect hedge.** It is exact only for infinitesimal moves, continuous trading, no costs
  and known volatility. Real hedges are rebalanced at intervals, leave gamma risk, and pay the spread each time.
- **Constant volatility is the weakest assumption.** The market quotes different implied volatilities for
  different strikes and maturities, which the next page takes up. The formula is kept as a quoting convention
  after its assumptions have been dropped.
