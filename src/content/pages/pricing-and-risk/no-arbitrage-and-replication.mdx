## IN ONE SENTENCE

If two portfolios pay exactly the same amount in every possible future, they must cost the same today, and nearly all
of derivative pricing is that sentence applied with care.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Before the 1970s an option was priced by estimating how likely it was to finish in the money and discounting the
expected payoff at a rate that reflected the risk. Every step needed a view, and two analysts with different views
quoted different prices with no way to say which was wrong.

The replication argument removes the view. It does not ask what the stock will do. It asks what *else* could be
bought that pays the same thing, and if that exists, the price of the second thing is the price of the first. A
dealer who quotes a different price can be traded against without risk, so the quote does not survive. This is why
the idea matters in practice: it gives a price that two people can check against each other, and it tells a dealer
what to buy to be safe after selling.

## DEFINITION

The **law of one price** says that two assets with identical payoffs in every state of the world must have the same
price. If they did not, one could buy the cheap one, sell the dear one and keep the difference with nothing left to
pay later. A market with no such opportunity is said to be free of arbitrage (see the glossary entry on
no-arbitrage). The law holds only as far as the two payoffs are really identical, and costs, financing and
counterparty risk are part of what makes them so or not.

Using it needs a way to build one payoff out of others. There are two.

**Static replication** builds the target payoff once, from instruments held until the end. Nothing is traded in
between. The standard example is **put-call parity**: a long call and a short put with the same strike and expiry
pay, at expiry, the underlying price minus the strike, whatever the underlying did. That is the payoff of a forward
agreed at that strike, so the combination must be worth what that forward is worth today. Static replication is
robust because it needs no model of how the price moves.

**Dynamic replication** builds the target by trading, adjusting the holdings as the price and the time to expiry
change. A call can be copied by holding some of the underlying and borrowing, with the amount held changing as the
market moves. Its price is the cost of the copy, plus nothing for a view. It rests on being able to trade as often
as the model assumes, at the prices the model assumes, which is where real markets part from it: the copy is
imperfect and the difference is a risk the seller keeps (this is the subject of the next page).

The third idea is a change of bookkeeping. If a price can be written as an expectation of a payoff, the expectation
has to be taken with weights that make the arbitrage-free prices come out right. Those weights form the
**risk-neutral measure**. They are not forecasts of what is likely to happen; they are the set of weights that
reproduces today's prices of the traded instruments, so that the price of anything new, built from those, follows.
Every price is also quoted in some unit, and the choice of unit, the **numéraire**, changes the weights that are
needed. The usual choice is cash growing at the riskless rate, but a bond or another asset can serve, and some
problems become easier with a different one.

## FORMALLY

Take one period. A stock is worth $S_0$ today and $S_u$ or $S_d$ at the end, with $S_d < S_0 e^{rT} < S_u$, and
cash earns the riskless rate $r$ over a period of length $T$. A claim pays $X_u$ or $X_d$. Hold $\Delta$ shares and
an amount $B$ of cash such that

$$
\Delta S_u + B e^{rT} = X_u, \qquad \Delta S_d + B e^{rT} = X_d ,
$$

two equations in two unknowns, solved by $\Delta = (X_u - X_d)/(S_u - S_d)$. The price of the claim is the cost of
the copy, $V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B$. Substituting and collecting terms gives

$$
V_0 = e^{-rT}\left[ q\,X_u + (1-q)\,X_d \right], \qquad q = \frac{S_0 e^{rT} - S_d}{S_u - S_d} .
$$

The number $q$ lies strictly between 0 and 1 exactly when the no-arbitrage condition above holds, so it behaves
like a probability, but the real-world probability of an up move appears nowhere. Under these weights, denoted
$\mathbb{Q}$, the stock's expected growth is the riskless rate. Equivalently, the price discounted by the
numéraire is a martingale:

$$
\frac{V_0}{N_0} = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[ \frac{V_T}{N_T} \right],
$$

with $N_t = e^{rt}$ for the cash numéraire. Choosing another positive traded asset as $N$ changes $\mathbb{Q}$ and
leaves $V_0$ unchanged.

A small numerical case, with $r=0$ for simplicity: $S_0 = 100$, $S_u = 120$, $S_d = 80$ and a call struck at 100
paying 20 or 0. Then $\Delta = 0.5$, $B = -40$ and $V_0 = 50 - 40 = 10$; also $q = 0.5$ and $V_0 = 0.5 \times 20 = 10$.
Changing the real-world chance of an up move from 30% to 70% changes the expected payoff and does not change the
price.

The general statement is the fundamental theorem of asset pricing: in a market model with suitable technical
conditions there is no arbitrage if and only if at least one such $\mathbb{Q}$ exists, and the market is complete,
meaning every payoff can be replicated, if and only if it is unique.

Put-call parity for European options on a stock paying no dividends is

$$
C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT} ,
$$

and in terms of the forward price $F$ for the same expiry, $C - P = e^{-rT}(F - K)$, which also covers dividends,
borrowing costs and index options. For American options parity holds only as an inequality, because early exercise
is worth something.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Choosing a frame in which the drift vanishes">
Changing from real-world probabilities to the risk-neutral ones changes the drift of the price process and leaves
its volatility alone, in the way a change to a freely falling frame removes a uniform force without altering the
relative motion of what is inside it. The pricing problem becomes simpler in the new frame, and the answer, being
about differences of traded assets, does not depend on which frame was used. The choice of numéraire is a further
choice of units.

The analogy stops at interpretation. The risk-neutral weights are a computational device fixed by prices, not a
description of how the world behaves, and a price calculated with them says nothing about the true chance of an
outcome. The frame is also available only if the instruments needed to replicate really trade.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Risk-neutral probabilities are not forecasts.** They are the weights that make today's prices consistent, and
  they put more weight on bad outcomes than the real-world chance when investors pay extra for protection. Reading
  them as predictions mixes up the price of risk with the amount of it.
- **No arbitrage does not mean prices are right.** It says that related prices are consistent with each other. A
  stock can be wildly mispriced and the options on it still satisfy parity with the stock exactly.
- **Replication is not free of risk when it is dynamic.** The copy needs continuous trading, known volatility and no
  costs. In practice there is a residual, and a price from a replication argument is a cost of hedging, not a
  guarantee of a profit.
- **Parity violations are rarely arbitrages.** Apparent gaps usually come from dividends not accounted for, the
  cost of borrowing the stock, early exercise on American options, stale quotes or the bid-ask spread. These are
  the first things to check before concluding anything.

- Hull, *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*, the chapters on the properties of stock options (put-call parity
  and its American-option bounds) and on the binomial model (no-arbitrage pricing and risk-neutral valuation).
- Shreve, *Stochastic Calculus for Finance I: The Binomial Asset Pricing Model*, chapters 1 and 2, for replication
  and the risk-neutral measure in the setting above, with the proofs.
- Björk, *Arbitrage Theory in Continuous Time*, the chapters on the martingale approach and on change of
  numéraire, for the general statements.
- Paper: Harrison and Pliska, *Martingales and Stochastic Integrals in the Theory of Continuous Trading* (1981,
  *Stochastic Processes and their Applications*), which connects absence of arbitrage, equivalent martingale
  measures and completeness.
