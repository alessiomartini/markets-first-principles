## IN ONE SENTENCE

Portfolio construction is choosing how much of each asset to hold, and the classical recipe for it amplifies exactly
the part of the data that is noise.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Before 1952 the standard advice was to pick the assets with the best prospects and spread the money around for
safety. Nobody had said what "spread around" was worth. If two assets move together, holding both buys little
protection; if they move apart, holding both can make the portfolio calmer than either. The quantity that matters
is therefore not the risk of each asset but how the risks add up, which depends on the correlations between them.

Harry Markowitz turned that observation into an optimisation problem: given expectations and covariances, find
the weights that give the least risk for a chosen expected return. It became the foundation of the field and
earned him a share of the 1990 Nobel prize in economics. It also posed a problem that practitioners met as soon as
they tried to use it. The inputs are not known, they are estimated, and the output is very sensitive to them.
Most of the later history of the subject is about what to do with that sensitivity.

## DEFINITION

**Markowitz optimisation**, also called mean-variance optimisation, chooses portfolio weights to maximise
expected return for a given variance, or to minimise variance for a given expected return, using a vector of
expected returns and a covariance matrix as inputs. The set of portfolios that cannot be improved on in this sense
is the **efficient frontier**: for each level of risk, the highest expected return available, drawn as a curve in
the plane of volatility and expected return.

The usual yardstick for a position on that plane is the **Sharpe ratio**, expected excess return divided by
volatility. The portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio, the tangency portfolio, is the one that, combined with
borrowing or lending at the risk-free rate, dominates every other choice in the mean-variance framework.

Practitioners split a portfolio's return into pieces that can be bought separately. **Beta** is the exposure to
the market, the slope of the portfolio's return on the market's return. A **factor** is a broader version of the
same idea: a common source of return, such as the market, company size, value or momentum, to which many assets
are exposed and for which a premium is paid or at least claimed. **Alpha** is what remains: the return not
explained by the exposures to those factors. Building a portfolio then means choosing how much of each exposure to
hold, and what is left over is the bet that the manager is paid for.

## FORMALLY

Let $w$ be the vector of weights, $\mu$ the vector of expected returns, $\Sigma$ the covariance matrix and $\lambda$
a risk-aversion parameter. The problem

$$
\max_{w}\; w^\top \mu \;-\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\, w^\top \Sigma\, w \qquad \text{subject to } \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1
$$

has, ignoring further constraints, the solution

$$
w^\star = \frac{1}{\lambda}\,\Sigma^{-1}\left(\mu - \gamma\,\mathbf{1}\right),
$$

where $\gamma$ is the multiplier that enforces the budget constraint. The covariance matrix enters through its
inverse. Write $\Sigma = \sum_i \lambda_i v_i v_i^\top$ in its eigenbasis. Then

$$
\Sigma^{-1} = \sum_i \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\, v_i v_i^\top ,
$$

so the directions in which the matrix is smallest receive the largest weight. The condition number
$\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ measures how badly an error in the inputs is magnified in the weights.

With $N$ assets and $T$ observations, the smallest eigenvalues of the sample covariance matrix are biased
downwards and the largest upwards, and the bias grows as $N/T$ approaches one. The optimiser is then asked to
exploit low-variance directions that exist in the sample and not in the world. The expected returns are worse
still: the error of a sample mean is of order $\sigma/\sqrt{T}$, which for equity returns over a few years is large
compared with the differences in true expected returns that the optimiser is trying to rank.

A factor model reduces the number of parameters. Writing the return of asset $i$ as

$$
r_i = \alpha_i + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{ik}\, f_k + \varepsilon_i ,
$$

with $K \ll N$ factors $f_k$ and uncorrelated residuals $\varepsilon_i$, the covariance matrix becomes
$\Sigma = B\,\Sigma_f\,B^\top + D$ with $D$ diagonal. That has on the order of $NK$ parameters instead of $N^2/2$,
which is the reason such models are used to build the inputs.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Inverting an ill-conditioned operator">
Mean-variance optimisation is an inverse problem. The covariance matrix is an operator, the expected returns are
the data, and the weights are the solution of a linear system. When the operator has eigenvalues spread over a
wide range, the inverse amplifies noise along the weakest directions, which is the standard behaviour of an
ill-conditioned problem, and the standard remedy is regularisation: shrink the small eigenvalues up, cut off the
modes that cannot be told from noise, or add a penalty on the size of the weights. A factor model is the same
remedy seen from the other side, because it imposes a low-rank structure and so removes the weak directions by
construction.

The analogy does not stretch to the data. In an inverse problem in physics the operator is usually known and the
noise is in the measurement. Here the operator itself is estimated from the same finite sample, and the series
are not stationary, so the weak directions of next year's matrix are not the weak directions of last year's.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **The efficient frontier is not a property of the market.** It is a property of an estimate. The curve computed
  from one sample is not the curve that holds in the next, and the gap between them is the object of study.
- **Diversification is not the same as holding many assets.** What reduces risk is holding assets whose returns are
  not driven by the same factor. A hundred stocks that all load on the market have the risk of the market.
- **A high Sharpe ratio in a backtest is not an expected Sharpe ratio.** The Sharpe ratio of the optimised weights
  computed in the same sample that chose them is biased upwards, because the weights were chosen to make it high.
- **Alpha is relative to a model.** Return called alpha under one set of factors may be a factor premium under a
  broader set. Calling something alpha is a statement about what the model leaves out, not a finding that it is
  skill.
- **Equal weights are not naive in a technical sense.** They ignore the inputs entirely, so they carry no
  estimation error from them, which is the reason they are a hard benchmark to beat when the sample is short.
