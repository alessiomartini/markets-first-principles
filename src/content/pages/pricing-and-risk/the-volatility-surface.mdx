## IN ONE SENTENCE

If the Black-Scholes formula were true, every option on the same underlying would imply the same volatility; they do not,
and the way they differ is what the market actually prices.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The previous page ended with a formula that has one free parameter, the volatility, and a convention of quoting
option prices in that unit. If the assumptions behind the formula held, inverting it would give the same number
for every strike and every maturity of the same underlying, because there is only one volatility to find.

Option markets never behaved like that for long. The standard account is that the pattern changed in a lasting way
after the stock market crash of October 1987: before it, implied volatilities for index options were roughly flat
across strikes, and afterwards low-strike puts were consistently more expensive in volatility terms than high-strike
calls. Whatever the exact date, the practical problem is permanent. A trader who must price an option that is not
quoted, or hedge a book of options at many strikes, cannot use one volatility. The volatility surface is the table
the market uses instead, and the models on this page are ways of making that table consistent.

## DEFINITION

Fix an underlying and a day. For every listed strike and expiry, take the option's price and invert the
Black-Scholes formula for the implied volatility. Plotted against strike and maturity, these numbers form a
surface, the **volatility surface**. Two slices of it have names.

The **volatility smile** is the profile of implied volatility across strike at one maturity. For currencies it is
often roughly U-shaped, which gives it the name. The **volatility skew** is the tilt of that profile. For equity
indices the dominant feature is a skew, with implied volatility highest at low strikes and falling as the strike
rises, which means that protection against falls is priced at a premium.

The **term structure of volatility** is the profile of implied volatility across maturity, typically read at the
money. It is usually upward-sloping when markets are calm and inverts, with short maturities above long ones, in a
crisis, when the market expects the present turbulence to fade.

Two families of models reproduce a given surface. A **local volatility** model keeps one source of randomness but
lets volatility depend on the price level and on time, $\sigma(S,t)$, chosen so that the model returns exactly the
quoted prices. A **stochastic volatility** model makes volatility a second random process, correlated with the
price, so that the surface comes out of the dynamics instead of being fitted to it.

The **VIX** is the Cboe's index of expected 30-day volatility of the S&P 500, computed from a strip of
out-of-the-money index options of the two nearest maturities around thirty days. Since its 2003 redefinition it is
read off the option prices directly, without inverting the Black-Scholes formula, and it is quoted in annualised
percentage points.

## FORMALLY

Write $C(K,T)$ for the price of a European call with strike $K$ and expiry $T$. Taking the second derivative with
respect to strike recovers the risk-neutral density of the underlying at expiry (Breeden and Litzenberger, 1978):

$$
q_T(K) = e^{rT}\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}.
$$

A whole surface of call prices is therefore the same information as a family of risk-neutral distributions, one per
maturity. A flat implied volatility gives a lognormal density; a skew gives a density with a heavier left tail than
lognormal, which is the statement about priced crash risk in a different form.

For a local volatility model $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma(S,t)S\,dW$ there is exactly one function $\sigma(S,t)$ that
reproduces a given arbitrage-free surface (Dupire, 1994). With zero rates and dividends it is

$$
\sigma^2(K,T) = \frac{2\,\partial C/\partial T}{K^2\,\partial^2 C/\partial K^2},
$$

and with a positive rate the numerator gains the term $rK\,\partial C/\partial K$. The formula makes plain what the model
is: a re-expression of the surface in the language of dynamics, not an explanation of it. It also has a known flaw,
which is that the future smiles it implies are flatter than those seen in practice.

A stochastic volatility model, of which Heston (1993) is the standard example, adds a second equation,

$$
dS = rS\,dt + \sqrt{v}\,S\,dW^{S}, \qquad dv = \kappa(\theta - v)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v}\,dW^{v}, \qquad d\langle W^{S},W^{v}\rangle=\rho\,dt .
$$

A negative correlation $\rho$ produces a skew, because falls in the price come with rises in volatility, and the
volatility of volatility $\xi$ controls how curved the smile is.

For the VIX, with $T$ the time to expiry, $F$ the forward price, $K_0$ the first strike below $F$ and $Q(K)$ the
mid-price of the out-of-the-money option at strike $K$, the squared index is

$$
\mathrm{VIX}^2 = \frac{2}{T}\sum_i \frac{\Delta K_i}{K_i^2}\,e^{rT} Q(K_i) \;-\; \frac{1}{T}\left(\frac{F}{K_0}-1\right)^2 ,
$$

multiplied by $100^2$ after interpolating to a constant 30 days. The first term is a discretisation of the fair
strike of a variance swap, a contract that pays realised variance, and it holds whatever the volatility process is,
provided the price has no jumps.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Inverse problems and an effective description">
Going from option prices to a local volatility is an inverse problem: a function of two variables is inferred from
observations of its second derivatives, and that is why raw quotes must be smoothed before the formula is applied,
since taking two derivatives of noisy data amplifies the noise. The implied volatility itself is an effective
parameter, like an effective temperature or an effective coupling: a single number chosen so that an
over-simple model matches a more complicated reality at one point.

The analogy stops at the question of what the parameter means. An effective coupling can be derived from a
microscopic theory, whereas the surface is set by supply and demand for protection from people who are paid to
take the other side. The skew is partly a statement about the distribution of returns and partly a price paid for
insurance, and the two cannot be separated from option prices alone.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **A smile is not a mistake in the market.** The formula's constant-volatility assumption is wrong, and the smile is
  how the market corrects for it. Nobody is arbitraging a smile away, because a smile is not an arbitrage.
- **The VIX is not a forecast of the S&P 500's direction.** It measures the price of 30-day variance and so
  includes the premium paid for protection, which is why it tends to sit above subsequently realised volatility.
  It rises when the index falls because investors buy protection, not because it predicts further falls.
- **Local volatility and stochastic volatility are not rival descriptions of the same fact.** Both can fit
  today's surface exactly or nearly so. They disagree about how the surface moves tomorrow and about the price
  of options whose payoff depends on the path, and that disagreement is where the models are tested.
- **Skew is a feature of the risk-neutral distribution, not necessarily of the real one.** A fat left tail in the
  risk-neutral density can come from the real probability of crashes, from the price of bearing that risk, or from
  both.
