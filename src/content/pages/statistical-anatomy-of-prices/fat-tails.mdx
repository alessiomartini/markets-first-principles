## IN ONE SENTENCE

The probability of a large price move decays as a power of its size, not exponentially, so moves that a Gaussian
would call impossible happen several times a decade.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The Gaussian is not an arbitrary default. If returns over a day were the sum of many small independent shocks, the
central limit theorem would deliver a Gaussian, and this page would not exist. So the observed fat tails are direct
evidence that at least one of those assumptions fails.

Both do. Shocks are not independent — volatility clusters, so large moves arrive in bursts rather than in isolation.
And they are not identically distributed with finite scale — the amount of information and forced liquidation
arriving at the market is itself wildly variable. What survives is a distribution whose body looks unremarkable and
whose tail is heavy enough to dominate every quantity that matters: the average loss on your worst days, the size of
the **drawdown** you must survive, the capital a regulator makes you hold.

## DEFINITION

A distribution has **fat tails** when the probability of large outcomes decays as a power law rather than
exponentially. The sharpest summary is the **tail exponent** $\alpha$, defined by how the survival function behaves
far from the centre: it fixes which moments exist, since moments of order $\alpha$ and above are infinite.

The cruder summary is **kurtosis**, the normalised fourth moment, with the Gaussian value subtracted so that zero
means "as fat as a Gaussian". Daily equity returns routinely show excess kurtosis of 10 or more. Its weakness is
that it is one number driven by a handful of observations, and if $\alpha \le 4$ it does not exist at all — which is
uncomfortably close to what is measured.

The first serious proposal for the tails, Mandelbrot's in the 1960s, was the **Lévy distribution**: the family
stable under addition, of which the Gaussian is the finite-variance special case. The modern verdict is that this
was the right idea and the wrong fit. Measured exponents cluster near 3, outside the stable range $\alpha < 2$, so
returns are fat-tailed but do have finite variance.

Two risk measures make the consequences concrete. **Value at risk** is a quantile of the loss distribution — the
loss not exceeded with probability $p$ over a stated horizon. A **VaR breach** is a day on which the realised loss
exceeded it. Fat tails do not merely make breaches more common; they make them *clustered* and *deep*, and VaR by
construction says nothing about depth.

## FORMALLY

The tail is a power law if the survival function is asymptotically scale-free:

$$
P\left(|r| > x\right) \;\underset{x \to \infty}{\sim}\; C x^{-\alpha}
$$

Taking logarithms, $\ln P(|r| > x) = \ln C - \alpha \ln x$, so a power-law tail is a straight line on log–log axes
with slope $-\alpha$. This is the whole reason the figure below uses those axes: the diagnostic is linearity, and
the estimate is a slope.

The empirical finding, robust across markets, decades and asset classes, is the *inverse cubic law*:

$$
\alpha \approx 3
$$

This value sits at an awkward place. Since $\alpha > 2$, the variance exists and **volatility** is a meaningful
quantity. Since $\alpha \approx 3 < 4$, the fourth moment does not converge, so measured **kurtosis** grows with
sample size instead of settling — which is exactly what is observed, and why kurtosis is a bad statistic to argue
with.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Critical exponents, and which fixed point you flow to">
A power-law tail with no characteristic scale is the same object as a critical correlation function, and it should
be estimated with the same discipline: fit the tail, never the body, state the fit range, and check the estimate is
stable as the range moves. Hill estimators are the standard tool and are as sensitive to threshold choice as any
critical exponent fit.

The deeper connection is to the classification of limit laws. Summing many independent random variables is a
renormalisation-group flow with two fixed points: the Gaussian when the variance is finite, and the Lévy stable laws
when it is not. Mandelbrot's proposal was that returns flow to the second. The data say something more interesting —
returns are *not* independent, so neither fixed point governs them cleanly, and the aggregation towards Gaussianity
happens far more slowly than independence would predict. That anomalously slow convergence is the subject of the
scale invariance page, and it is a stronger signal than the tail exponent itself.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Fat tails are not "more volatility".** Volatility is a scale parameter; tails are a shape. A series can have
  identical volatility and a completely different probability of ruin.
- **"Six-sigma event" is a statement about a model, not about the world.** Under a power law with $\alpha \approx 3$
  a six-sigma daily move is a roughly once-a-decade occurrence, not a once-in-the-age-of-the-universe one. Reaching
  for the phrase is usually an admission that the Gaussian was assumed.
- **A fat tail is not a fat body.** The distribution near the centre is unremarkable and often looks Gaussian, which
  is why in-sample fit statistics dominated by the body will happily accept the wrong model.
- **Kurtosis is not the tail exponent.** They point the same way but kurtosis may not exist, and it is dominated by
  a few points. If a claim about tails rests on a kurtosis number, it rests on about five observations.
