## IN ONE SENTENCE

Prices are unpredictable enough that almost nothing beats costs, and structured enough that the unpredictability
itself demands explanation.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The efficient market hypothesis is usually presented as a claim to be believed or rejected, which is the least
useful way to hold it. As stated it cannot be tested at all: any test of "prices reflect information" requires a
model of what returns *should* be, so a rejection can always be blamed on the model rather than on efficiency. This
is Fama's own joint-hypothesis problem, and it is fatal to the question in its grand form.

The question becomes productive when narrowed to something operational. Not "are markets efficient" but: how large
is the predictable component, at what horizon does it live, how much capital can exploit it before **market
impact** eats the edge, and does it survive costs? These have answers, they are measurable, and the answers are
mostly "small, short, little, and barely" — which is a far more informative statement than either slogan.

## DEFINITION

The **efficient market hypothesis** comes in three strengths: weak (past prices are already reflected), semi-strong
(all public information), strong (private information too). The practitioner's version replaces all three with a
question about net-of-cost exploitability.

**No-arbitrage** is the much weaker and much more useful condition that no portfolio can cost nothing, never lose,
and sometimes gain. It requires nobody to be rational or informed, only that free money is taken quickly — which is
why almost all of derivative pricing rests on it and is correspondingly robust.

The **variance ratio** compares variance measured over long horizons with variance over short ones, scaled so a
random walk gives one. Plotted against lag it is the *signature plot*, and its shape diagnoses trending or
mean-reversion.

Two findings sit awkwardly against efficiency. **Excess volatility** is Shiller's observation that prices move far
more than any plausible fundamental. The **diffusivity puzzle** is Track 2's version: **order flow** is strongly
autocorrelated while prices are nearly a martingale, which should be impossible.

## FORMALLY

The martingale statement is the testable core:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t\right] = S_t
$$

and it implies that variance grows linearly in time, so the variance ratio

$$
\text{VR}(\tau) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(r_{t \to t+\tau}\right)}{\tau \operatorname{Var}\left(r_{t \to t+1}\right)}
$$

equals one at every lag. Empirically it is close to one over most horizons — the strongest evidence for efficiency
there is — and departs at both ends: below one at the shortest lags, largely because trade prices bounce between
**bid** and **ask** rather than because of any economic mechanism, and mildly below one over multi-year horizons,
consistent with slow mean reversion.

The **diffusivity puzzle** sharpens the tension. Order flow has autocorrelation $C_\varepsilon(\tau) \sim
\tau^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma$ small, so flow is highly predictable. If each trade moved the price by a fixed amount,
prices would inherit that predictability and $\text{VR}$ would exceed one substantially. It does not, because
impact decays through the **propagator** with exponent $\beta \approx (1-\gamma)/2$, and that exponent is exactly
what makes the predictable component cancel. Efficiency here is not an assumption but an *outcome*, enforced
dynamically by liquidity providers who would otherwise be picked off.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Detailed balance, and efficiency as a dynamical outcome">
The closest analogue is detailed balance: the assertion is not that nothing happens, but that no exploitable
*current* flows. A market can be full of structure — clustered volatility, persistent order flow, sector
correlations — while offering no net direction anyone can extract, exactly as a system in equilibrium is full of
microscopic motion with no macroscopic flux.

What makes the financial version more interesting than the physical one is that the balance is actively maintained
rather than given. There is no conservation law forcing $\text{VR} = 1$; it holds because participants remove
deviations, and the propagator exponent relation is the quantitative trace of that removal. Efficiency is therefore
a fixed point of a dynamical system with competing agents, which predicts something a static assumption does not:
the balance should fail transiently when the mechanism that maintains it is impaired. That is precisely what is
observed in crises, when liquidity providers withdraw and prices become both more volatile and more predictable at
once.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Efficient does not mean correct.** Prices can be unpredictable and still far from any sensible fundamental
  value — **excess volatility** is exactly that combination.
- **A predictable pattern is not an opportunity.** Costs, impact and capacity stand between a statistical finding
  and a profit, and they eliminate most findings.
- **Rejecting EMH requires a model of expected returns.** Without one, any anomaly can be relabelled a risk premium.
  This is why the debate is unresolvable in its grand form.
- **Efficiency is not a property markets have.** It is a state they are pushed toward by participants, and it fails
  exactly when those participants stop pushing.
