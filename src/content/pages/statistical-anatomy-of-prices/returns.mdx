## IN ONE SENTENCE

The quantity that behaves well statistically is not the price but the change in its logarithm, and almost every
tool in this track assumes you have already made that substitution.

## WHY IT EXISTS

A price series is a terrible object to do statistics on. It is non-stationary — its mean and variance depend on
when you look — and its scale is arbitrary, so a one-pound move means something different for a five-pound stock
than for a five-hundred-pound one. Comparing two assets, or one asset across decades, requires normalising away
both problems.

Proportional changes fix the scale problem. Taking logarithms fixes the second, subtler one: it makes changes
*additive*, so that a sequence of moves is a sum rather than a product. That single property is what makes the
entire apparatus of sums of random variables — central limit theorems, cumulants, scaling arguments, the whole of
Track 3 — applicable at all.

## DEFINITION

The **simple return** is the proportional change, $R_t = S_t/S_{t-1} - 1$. It is what happened to your money, and
it is the right quantity for reporting performance.

The **log return** is $r_t = \ln S_t - \ln S_{t-1}$. It is the right quantity for statistics, because it has
**additivity**: the return over $n$ periods is the sum of the $n$ one-period returns, whereas simple returns
compound. Every model in this track is written in log returns, and this site writes $r_t$ for log and $R_t$ for
simple whenever both appear.

**Drift** is the deterministic part of the motion — the $\mu$ that survives averaging. It is the quantity everyone
wants and almost nobody can measure.

**Annualisation** rescales a figure to a yearly basis: returns multiply by the number of periods, volatilities by
the square root of it. The square root is not a convention but a consequence of additivity plus independence, which
means it is slightly wrong in practice, since returns are neither perfectly independent nor homoskedastic.

## FORMALLY

The two definitions are related exactly by

$$
r_t = \ln\left(1 + R_t\right)
$$

so they agree to first order for small moves and diverge for large ones — always in the same direction, since
$\ln(1+x) < x$. A $-50\%$ simple return is a log return of $-0.69$; a $+100\%$ simple return is $+0.69$. The
asymmetry of simple returns, where losses and gains of the same magnitude are not symmetric, disappears in logs.

Additivity is the property that matters:

$$
r_{t \to t+n} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} r_{t+k}, \qquad
R_{t \to t+n} = \prod_{k=1}^{n} \left(1 + R_{t+k}\right) - 1
$$

If the $r_t$ were independent with variance $\sigma^2$, the variance of the sum would be $n\sigma^2$, giving the
square-root scaling behind **annualisation**:

$$
\sigma_{\text{ann}} = \sigma_{\text{daily}} \sqrt{252}
$$

This is exact under independence and approximately right in practice, and the ways it fails are the subject of the
next four pages. There is one genuine trap: because $\mathbb{E}[r] = \mathbb{E}[\ln(1+R)] < \ln(1 + \mathbb{E}[R])$
by Jensen's inequality, the average log return is systematically below the log of the average simple return. The
gap is $\sigma^2/2$, and it is not an artefact — it is the difference between the typical outcome and the mean
outcome of a multiplicative process, which is exactly what the Kelly criterion in Track 4 is about.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Choosing the variable in which the dynamics are simple">
This is a change of variables chosen so that the equation of motion becomes additive, and it is the same move as
working with the logarithm of a multiplicative process anywhere in physics. A price is generated multiplicatively —
each period scales the previous level — so the natural coordinate is $\ln S$, in which the process becomes a sum of
increments and therefore a candidate for every limit theorem there is.

The Jensen gap above is worth dwelling on, because it is the same distinction as between the typical and the mean
value of a random multiplicative variable. For a log-normal quantity the mean is dominated by rare large
realisations and is not where the distribution lives; the median tracks $\exp$ of the mean log instead. In finance
this is the difference between the growth rate you will actually experience and the expected value of your wealth —
two numbers that diverge by $\sigma^2/2$ per period, and confusing them is how leveraged strategies get sold.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Log returns do not average like simple returns.** A portfolio's return is a weighted average of its holdings'
  *simple* returns, not of their log returns. Use logs for time series, simple for cross-sections.
- **Annualising by $\sqrt{252}$ assumes independence.** Since volatility clusters and returns mean-revert slightly,
  annualised volatility is systematically off, and the error is not always in the same direction.
- **A price chart on a linear axis is misleading by construction.** Equal vertical distances are unequal
  proportional moves, so the recent past always looks more dramatic. Log axes are the default for a reason.
- **The mean return is not the growth rate.** They differ by $\sigma^2/2$, and the difference grows with
  volatility. This is why a strategy can have positive expected return and still take you to zero.
