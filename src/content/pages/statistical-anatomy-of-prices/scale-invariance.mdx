## IN ONE SENTENCE

The shape of the return distribution depends on the horizon you measure it over, and how it changes with horizon
tells you more than the shape at any one horizon does.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The previous two pages each described a distribution at a fixed scale: fat tails in daily returns, clustered
volatility in daily magnitudes. Neither says what happens if you change the scale, and that turns out to be the
more informative question.

The reason is that a central limit theorem gives a hard prediction. If returns were independent, summing $n$ of
them would push the distribution toward a Gaussian at a known rate — excess kurtosis falling exactly as $1/n$.
Measuring the actual rate is therefore a test with a sharp null hypothesis, and the test fails. Convergence happens,
but far too slowly, and the shortfall is a direct measurement of dependence that no single-scale statistic can
provide.

## DEFINITION

**Aggregational Gaussianity** is the observation that longer-horizon returns look more normal: daily returns are
strongly **fat tails**, monthly less so, annual nearly Gaussian. Its presence is unremarkable; its *rate* is the
finding.

The **Hurst exponent** $H$ describes how the typical size of a move grows with the horizon, $\mathbb{E}|r_\tau| \sim
\tau^H$. A memoryless random walk gives $H = 1/2$, trending gives more, mean-reversion less. Measured values for
prices sit very close to $1/2$ — with the honest caveat that $H$ estimators are badly biased in short samples, so
small departures deserve suspicion rather than excitement.

**Multifractality** is the finding that a single exponent does not suffice. Different moments scale with different
exponents, so the scaling function $\zeta(q)$ is a curve rather than the straight line a simple self-affine process
would give. That curvature is the sharpest quantitative signature linking financial series to turbulent cascades.

## FORMALLY

Define the scaling of the $q$-th absolute moment across horizons:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left|r_\tau\right|^q \;\sim\; \tau^{\zeta(q)}
$$

For a random walk with finite variance, $\zeta(q) = q/2$ — a straight line through the origin with slope $H = 1/2$.
This is the null hypothesis, and departures from it are what "multifractal" names.

Empirically $\zeta$ is concave, well fitted by

$$
\zeta(q) = \frac{q}{2} - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\, q\left(q - 2\right)
$$

with the *intermittency coefficient* $\lambda^2$ measured at around $0.02$ to $0.05$ for equity indices. Setting
$\lambda^2 = 0$ recovers the monofractal walk, so a single small number measures the whole departure from
independence.

The same parameter controls the convergence rate the figure measures. Under independence, excess kurtosis of
$n$-aggregated returns falls as

$$
\kappa_n = \frac{\kappa_1}{n}
$$

whereas a multifractal process gives a decay slower than $1/n$, approaching Gaussian only logarithmically slowly.
The gap between the measured curve and $1/n$ is thus a direct, model-light measurement of dependence — and it is the
same dependence that appears as **long memory** in the previous page, seen from a different direction.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A renormalisation-group flow that has not converged">
Aggregating returns is a coarse-graining operation, and asking what the distribution flows to is a
renormalisation-group question. For independent increments with finite variance the flow reaches the Gaussian fixed
point quickly. For independent increments with infinite variance it reaches a Lévy stable fixed point. Real returns
do neither: they crawl toward the Gaussian at a rate that no fixed point with independent increments predicts,
because the increments are not independent and the dependence itself is scale-free.

This is why $\zeta(q)$ is the right object to measure rather than the distribution at any one scale. A whole
function of exponents, rather than a single one, is precisely the signature of a system with no characteristic
scale and a continuum of local scaling behaviours — the same diagnostic used for multifractal measures in
turbulence, where $\lambda^2$ plays the role of the intermittency parameter. The multifractal random walk model
imports that construction wholesale and reproduces both this page's curvature and the previous page's power-law
memory from one mechanism.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Aggregational Gaussianity does not rescue the Gaussian.** Convergence is far too slow to justify normality at
  the horizons risk is actually measured over.
- **$H \ne 1/2$ is usually an estimation artefact.** Hurst estimators are biased in short samples and sensitive to
  trends; most published claims of long-range dependence in *returns* do not survive careful testing. In absolute
  returns, the dependence is real.
- **Multifractality is not the same as fat tails.** Tails describe one scale; multifractality describes how the
  description changes across scales. A process can have fat tails and be perfectly monofractal.
- **Scaling laws are not universal constants.** The intermittency coefficient varies by asset and by era, and
  reading it as a fundamental number of markets is overreach.
