## IN ONE SENTENCE

Bad news moves prices and raises volatility; good news of the same size moves prices and does not.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The name is a fossil. The original explanation was mechanical: when a firm's equity value falls, its debt-to-equity
ratio rises, the equity becomes a more leveraged claim on the same assets, and its volatility should therefore
increase. The story is coherent and almost certainly not the main cause — the effect is far larger and far faster
than balance-sheet leverage can explain, and it appears in indices, commodities and assets with no leverage
whatsoever.

The better account is behavioural and mechanical in a different sense. Falling prices trigger risk limits, margin
calls and stop losses, all of which force selling, which moves prices further and raises measured volatility. Rising
prices trigger no equivalent forced buying. The asymmetry is built into the institutional plumbing of leverage
rather than into the accounting identity that gave the effect its name — which is why **panic asymmetry** is the
more honest label.

## DEFINITION

**Panic asymmetry** is the empirical fact that volatility responds more to falls than to equivalent rises. It is
measured as a negative correlation between today's return and future **realised volatility**, decaying over days to
weeks.

**Skewness** is the related property of the return distribution itself: equity index returns are negatively skewed,
with large falls larger and more frequent than large rises. It is not universal — individual stocks are often
positively skewed and FX pairs are close to symmetric — which is exactly why this page is about specific markets
rather than about prices in general.

**Volatility of volatility** is the variability of volatility itself. It matters here because the leverage effect
is a statement about the *joint* dynamics of price and variance, and any model reproducing it needs variance to be
a stochastic process with its own risk, not a parameter.

## FORMALLY

The effect is measured by a lagged cross-correlation between returns and future squared returns,

$$
\mathcal{L}(\tau) \;=\; \frac{\left\langle r_t \, \left|r_{t+\tau}\right|^2 \right\rangle - \left\langle r_t \right\rangle \left\langle \left|r_{t+\tau}\right|^2 \right\rangle}{\left\langle \left|r_t\right|^2 \right\rangle^2}
$$

For equity indices $\mathcal{L}(\tau)$ is negative for $\tau > 0$ and decays roughly exponentially over one to two
weeks, while being close to zero for $\tau < 0$. That asymmetry in the *sign of the lag* is the crucial test: a
common driver moving both quantities at once would produce a symmetric function, whereas a causal response produces
one that is negative only in the forward direction.

The magnitude also separates markets sharply. Equity indices show a strong effect, individual stocks a weaker one,
and FX close to none — which makes sense on the plumbing account, since there is no direction in a currency pair
that constitutes "down" for everyone. A fall in EUR/USD is a rise in USD/EUR, and the two cannot both trigger the
same forced selling.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A broken symmetry, and how to test for causation">
The clean statement is that the joint dynamics of price and volatility break the symmetry $r \to -r$. The
magnitude process alone respects it, since $|r|$ is even; the coupling between level and variance does not. Asking
which symmetries a model must break is a more productive framing than asking which correlations it must fit,
because it tells you immediately that any model where volatility is driven by $|r|$ alone — including standard
**GARCH** — is structurally incapable of producing this effect and needs an asymmetric term added by hand.

The second transferable idea is the lag test. Comparing $\mathcal{L}(\tau)$ for $\tau > 0$ against $\tau < 0$ is a
test of causal ordering: a shared external driver appears symmetrically in lag, while a genuine response appears
only forward in time. This is the same reasoning as using time-asymmetry of a response function to distinguish
driving from correlation, and it is the strongest evidence available that this is a response rather than a common
cause.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **The name is wrong.** Balance-sheet leverage cannot account for the speed or size of the effect, and the effect
  appears where there is no leverage at all.
- **It is not universal across asset classes.** FX shows almost none, for a structural reason: there is no
  direction that counts as "down" for both sides of a pair.
- **Negative skewness and the leverage effect are different statements.** One is about the distribution at a single
  horizon, the other about the joint dynamics of two quantities over time. A process can have either without the
  other.
- **Standard GARCH cannot produce it.** Because variance responds to $r^2$, the sign is discarded. Reproducing this
  requires an explicitly asymmetric model.
