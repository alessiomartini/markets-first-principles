## IN ONE SENTENCE

Asset classes are a working taxonomy of tradable things, grouped by the kind of claim they represent rather than by
who issued them.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Nobody designed this partition; it accumulated. It survives because it tracks something real: assets within a class
tend to respond to the same shocks, so the classification is a crude first model of correlation. That is also its
main use — an investor deciding how to spread risk needs categories that fail at different times, and asset classes
are the first approximation to that.

It is worth being clear that the boundaries are conventions, not natural kinds. A convertible bond is debt that
turns into equity; a commodity-linked note is a bond whose payment depends on copper; a REIT is a share whose
underlying is property. Each sits awkwardly across a line. The taxonomy earns its keep anyway, because institutions
are organised around it — desks, mandates, indices, regulation and career paths all follow these lines, so the
categories shape behaviour even where they do not carve nature at the joints.

## DEFINITION

**Equity** is a residual claim on a company: last in the queue, unlimited upside, and worth zero before the company
itself is worthless. **Fixed income** is a claim to a contractually defined schedule of payments, which makes its
two questions sharp — what discount rate applies, and will the payments arrive.

**FX** is the exchange of one currency for another. It is the odd one out, because a quote is always a rate between
an ordered pair, so every price is simultaneously the inverse price of something else and there is no meaningful
notion of the asset "going up" on its own.

**Commodities** are physical raw materials traded in **standardisation** contracts. Their forward curves encode
**storage**, transport and seasonality rather than pure discounting, which makes them behave unlike anything
financial. **Real assets** — property, infrastructure, farmland — are the illiquid, appraisal-valued end of the same
physical idea.

**Crypto** is the newest entry and the one whose classification is most contested. As a financial object a token is
a claim with no cash flows, priced on what someone else will pay; what genuinely distinguishes it is the plumbing,
which is why it earns institutional attention out of proportion to its size.

## FORMALLY

Two numbers summarise a class over a sample: the annualised mean log return and the annualised volatility,

$$
\mu_{\text{ann}} = \frac{252}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} r_t, \qquad
\sigma_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{252} \cdot \operatorname{sd}(r_t)
$$

and their ratio is the Sharpe ratio, the currency in which allocations are argued about.

The asymmetry between these two estimates is the thing to internalise, and it is a pure statistics result rather
than a fact about finance. The standard error of the volatility estimate falls with the number of observations,
so a year of daily data pins it down usefully. The standard error of the mean does not improve with sampling
frequency at all — it depends only on the *span* of the sample:

$$
\operatorname{se}(\mu_{\text{ann}}) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{ann}}}{\sqrt{T_{\text{years}}}}
$$

With $\sigma_{\text{ann}} \approx 20\%$, distinguishing a 6% expected return from a 3% one at any reasonable
confidence needs decades of data. This is why the figure below separates the classes vertically and not
horizontally, and why almost every confident statement about which asset class "returns more" is weaker than it
sounds.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Measuring a drift against a diffusion">
This is the estimation problem of a Brownian particle with drift, and it has a well-known answer. The displacement
after time $T$ is $\mu T$ plus noise of size $\sigma\sqrt{T}$, so the signal-to-noise ratio grows only as
$\sqrt{T}$ — and crucially, sampling the path more finely does not help, because the noise you resolve grows just
as fast as the extra information.

Volatility is the opposite case: it is a property of the quadratic variation, which *is* recoverable from fine
sampling, and in the continuous limit is measurable exactly over an arbitrarily short window. A physicist meeting
finance for the first time should take this asymmetry as the governing constraint of the whole field. Risk is
measurable; expected return is barely estimable at all, and the entire apparatus of portfolio construction in
Track 4 is built around that imbalance.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **The classes are not equally sized.** Fixed income dwarfs equity by amount outstanding, and FX dwarfs both by
  turnover. Attention in the press runs almost exactly the other way round.
- **Diversification is a claim about correlation, not about counting.** Holding six asset classes that all fall
  together in a crisis is one position wearing six labels, and correlations rise precisely when you need them not
  to.
- **"Alternative" is a fee category, not an asset class.** It groups things by illiquidity and fee structure rather
  than by the kind of claim, which is why it can contain both farmland and a quant fund.
- **Low reported volatility can be an artefact of infrequent valuation.** **Real assets** are appraised quarterly;
  a price that is rarely observed cannot jump, and that smoothness is a property of the measurement rather than of
  the risk.

## GO DEEPER

- Ilmanen, *Expected Returns*, chapters 1–3. The best single survey of what each class has actually paid and why,
  and honest about the estimation problem above.
- Hull, *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*, chapter 1, for the standard institutional partition.
- Mishkin, *The Economics of Money, Banking and Financial Markets*, chapter 2, on why these markets exist at all.
- Paper: Dimson, Marsh & Staunton, *Triumph of the Optimists* — a century of returns across countries, and the
  clearest demonstration that the equity premium is a statement with very wide error bars.
