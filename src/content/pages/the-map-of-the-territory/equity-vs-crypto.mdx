## IN ONE SENTENCE

The same instrument type — a transferable claim traded on a screen — behaves differently depending on whether an
institution stands behind the record of who owns it.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Equity market structure is an accumulation of responses to failures. Disclosure rules exist because people sold
shares in companies that did not exist; custody and settlement rules exist because certificates were lost and
stolen; **CCP** clearing exists because counterparties defaulted. Every piece of apparatus that makes public equity
markets look slow and bureaucratic is scar tissue over a specific historical wound.

Crypto rebuilt the same functions — matching, clearing, custody, settlement — from scratch, with different trust
assumptions and without the scar tissue. That makes the comparison unusually instructive rather than merely
partisan: it is close to a controlled experiment in what each institution was actually for. The answer, so far, has
mostly been that the wounds recur.

## DEFINITION

A **listing** is admission of a security to trading on an **exchange**, bundled with continuing obligations:
audited accounts, disclosure of material information, governance and float standards. The obligations are the
product. They generate the information that everyone else's analysis consumes, and nothing equivalent gates a
token's appearance on a crypto venue.

**Free float** is the fraction of shares actually available to trade, excluding strategic holders who never sell.
It determines how much can be bought without moving the price, and it is the weighting used by most indices. A
**corporate action** — dividend, split, buyback, merger — is the issuer changing the terms of its own securities,
and it is why raw price series must be adjusted before any analysis is done on them.

**Custody** is the business of holding the asset and keeping the record of ownership. This is the sharpest fork. In
equities a custodian bank holds the position and its records are the legal proof. In crypto the function is either
a private key you hold yourself, with no counterparty and no recourse, or a **CEX** holding it for you — a single
company acting as exchange, broker, clearer and custodian at once, which securities regulation forbids precisely
because each role checks the others. A **DEX** avoids custody entirely by executing on-chain, usually against an
automated market maker rather than an **order book**.

**24/7 trading** is the last structural difference: crypto never closes, so it has no closing auction to produce an
official price and no overnight gap to concentrate the reaction to news.

## FORMALLY

Both markets produce a price series, but only one produces a canonical daily number. An equity's official close
comes from a call auction that maximises executed volume at a single price $p^\*$:

$$
p^\* = \arg\max_p \ \min\left(D(p),\, S(p)\right)
$$

This single print is what index funds rebalance against, what derivatives settle on, and what performance is
measured with. A continuous market has no such moment, so "the daily close" in crypto is an arbitrary timestamp,
and different venues disagree about it.

The consequence shows up in the returns themselves. Write the daily return of a market that closes as the sum of
an overnight and an intraday piece,

$$
r_{\text{daily}} = \underbrace{\ln \frac{O_t}{C_{t-1}}}_{\text{overnight gap}} + \underbrace{\ln \frac{C_t}{O_t}}_{\text{intraday}}
$$

For equities a substantial share of total variance sits in the first term — a jump across a period in which no
trading was possible. In crypto that term is identically zero by construction, and the same information arrives as
a continuous move instead.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Boundary conditions, not different physics">
It is tempting to treat these as two different systems. They are better understood as the same dynamics under
different boundary conditions. An exchange that closes imposes a periodic boundary in time: information accumulates
while the system cannot relax, and releases discontinuously at the open. A market that never closes has no such
boundary, so the same information is dissipated continuously.

This predicts what the figure below shows, and the prediction is a real one because it could have failed. Removing
the boundary does not remove the daily cycle — the participants still sleep, so activity still tracks the working
hours of the major financial centres. What changes is the *form* of the response, from a discontinuous jump to a
continuous relaxation, not the underlying periodicity. The clock is in the traders, not in the venue.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **"Decentralised" usually is not.** Most crypto volume trades on a handful of centralised venues. The
  architecture of the settlement layer says little about the concentration of the trading layer.
- **Market capitalisation is not money invested.** Multiplying the last price by total supply assumes every unit
  could be sold at that price, which is false in both markets and wildly so where **free float** is small.
- **Self-custody removes a counterparty, not a risk.** It replaces the custodian's failure mode with your own, and
  key loss is irreversible in a way that a custodian's error is not.
- **A 24-hour market is not a uniformly liquid one.** Depth still collapses when the major centres are asleep, and
  the largest crypto dislocations have consistently happened in those hours.
