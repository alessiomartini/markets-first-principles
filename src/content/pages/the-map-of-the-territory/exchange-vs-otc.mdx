## IN ONE SENTENCE

An exchange publishes a book that anyone can trade against; over-the-counter, you ask a dealer for a price and only
you find out what it was.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Public and bilateral trading solve opposite problems, which is why both survive.

A central book is the best available machine for discovering a price in a **standardisation** instrument with many
interested participants. It concentrates interest, publishes the result, and lets the crowd compete to supply
liquidity. But it has two failures. It cannot handle instruments that are not standard — no book can exist in a
swap tailored to one pension fund's liabilities. And it punishes size: displaying a large order in public tells
everyone what you intend to do, and they will price accordingly before you finish.

Bilateral dealing answers both. A **dealer** will quote a customised instrument, and will show a price in size
because only the two of you know about it. The price of that discretion is that you cannot see the market — you see
the quotes you were shown, and cannot tell whether they were good.

## DEFINITION

An **order-driven market** produces prices from participants' own orders meeting in a central **order book**. No one
is obliged to quote; the best price is whatever the crowd has posted. This is how listed equities and futures trade.

A **quote-driven market** produces prices from **dealer**s standing ready to buy and sell on their own account.
Customers trade against the dealer's book rather than against each other. Bonds, swaps and most **FX** work this
way, and the discovery mechanism is **RFQ** — asking several dealers for a price on a stated size and trading with
the best. RFQ contains its own tension: asking more dealers samples more prices and reveals your intention to more
people who could trade ahead of you.

Between the two extremes sit venues that are neither. A **dark pool** matches orders without displaying them first,
publishing only after the fact, which protects a large order from the impact that displaying it would cause.
**Internalisation** is a broker matching customer orders against its own inventory instead of routing them out —
how most US retail equity orders are executed, and the reason **retail flow** never reaches an exchange.

## FORMALLY

The distinction is about who bears **market impact** and who observes it. In a lit book, an order of size $Q$ walks
the resting depth and pays an average price worse than the touch by an amount set by the shape of the book, and
every other participant sees it happen. Empirically that cost grows as roughly the square root of size,

$$
\mathcal{I}(Q) \;\approx\; Y \sigma \sqrt{\frac{Q}{V}}
$$

with $V$ the daily volume, $\sigma$ the daily volatility, and $Y$ a constant of order one. The square root is the
important part: impact is concave, so large orders are penalised less than proportionally, but the penalty is
unavoidable and public.

In an RFQ the cost is instead the dealer's spread plus whatever the dealer charges for the risk of warehousing $Q$
until it can be unwound. The customer pays a negotiated price rather than a mechanical one, and the information
leaks to $n$ dealers rather than to the whole market. Choosing between the two is a genuine optimisation, not a
matter of taste — and it is why the same institution routes small orders to an exchange and large ones to a phone.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Observability, and the cost of measurement">
The interesting variable here is not price but information. A lit book is a fully observable system: the state is
published, and every interaction with it perturbs the state in a way everyone can see. Trading is therefore a
measurement that disturbs what it measures, and the disturbance is the cost.

Over-the-counter trading is the partially observable version. Your interaction perturbs the system too, but the
perturbation is visible only to the counterparties you chose to reveal it to, and in exchange you lose access to the
state. The design question — how many dealers to ask — is precisely a trade-off between how much of the state you
sample and how much you disturb by sampling it. It is worth resisting the temptation to push the quantum analogy
further than that; the mechanism is ordinary information leakage, and it needs no stranger physics than that.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **"Off-exchange" is not a synonym for dark.** Most off-exchange equity volume is **internalisation** at or inside
  the public quote, not anonymous block trading in a pool.
- **OTC is not smaller than exchange trading.** By notional it is much larger. Bonds, swaps and FX — the biggest
  markets there are — are predominantly bilateral.
- **A dealer quoting you a price is not your agent.** The dealer is your **counterparty** and profits from the other
  side of your trade. A **broker** is the agent. The same firm often does both, on different desks.
- **Dark pools are not unregulated.** In the US they are registered alternative trading systems that report volume
  to FINRA — which is why the figure above can exist at all.
