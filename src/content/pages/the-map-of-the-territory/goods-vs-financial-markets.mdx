## IN ONE SENTENCE

A financial asset is a promise you can hold indefinitely at almost no cost, and that single property changes
everything about how its price behaves.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Markets for physical goods came first, and finance inherited their vocabulary while quietly dropping their
constraints. A wheat merchant has to worry about where the grain sits, whether it spoils, what it costs to move,
and whether the sack he is offered is the same grade as the sack he was promised. None of these apply to a share:
it occupies no space, does not rot, moves by book entry, and is indistinguishable from every other share of its
class.

The distinction matters because those physical constraints are exactly what let — or prevent — arbitrage from
linking prices across time and place. Where an asset can be bought now and held, today's price and the expected
future price are tied together by a chain anyone can execute. Where it cannot, the chain is cut, and each moment
becomes its own market. Almost every strange-looking price in commodities is that cut chain showing itself.

## DEFINITION

A market trades **spot** when the exchange happens now, at today's price, as opposed to a contract fixing today the
terms of an exchange later. "Now" is a convention: spot equities settle a day or two later, spot FX in two business
days, spot crude on a schedule measured in weeks.

Whether that trade ends in **delivery** — the actual handover of the asset — or in a cash payment is the first fork
in the road. Physical delivery brings with it every question a financial contract avoids: where, in what grade, and
at whose cost.

Three properties of the underlying then determine how the price behaves. **Storage** is whether the asset can be
carried from one period into the next and at what cost; it is what allows a trader to buy now, hold, and sell
forward, tying the two prices together. **Perishability** is the limiting case where that is impossible.
**Fungibility** is whether any unit substitutes for any other, which is what allows a buyer to be matched with any
seller rather than with one particular counterparty.

**Standardisation** is the deliberate manufacture of fungibility: an exchange defines grade, quantity, location and
month so precisely that the contracts become interchangeable and can be traded in a single book. The cost of that
convenience is **basis risk** — a standard contract rarely matches anyone's exact exposure.

## FORMALLY

For a storable asset with no yield, holding it from now to $T$ costs interest plus storage, so a trader who buys
spot at $S_t$, stores, and sells forward locks in a riskless profit unless

$$
F(t,T) = S_t \, e^{(r + u)(T - t)}
$$

where $r$ is the financing rate and $u$ the storage cost per unit time. This is not a model of anyone's beliefs
about the future; it is an arbitrage identity, enforced by anyone with a warehouse.

The identity holds only in one direction when the asset cannot be borrowed or sold short, and it fails entirely when
storage is impossible. Setting $u \to \infty$ gives the perishable case:

$$
u \to \infty \;\Longrightarrow\; F(t,T) \ \text{unconstrained by} \ S_t
$$

For electricity, delivery in one hour and delivery in the next are effectively separate assets. Their prices need
not be related at all, and empirically they are not — which is why an electricity price can rise a hundredfold for
an hour and leave tomorrow untouched.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="A conserved quantity, and what happens when conservation fails">
Storability makes inventory a conserved quantity with a leak. Buy a barrel today and it is still there tomorrow,
minus a storage cost — so inventory couples adjacent time periods the way a capacitor couples charge to voltage, and
the price series inherits a memory it would not otherwise have. Shocks get smoothed across periods because inventory
absorbs them.

Remove storage and the coupling vanishes. Each delivery period becomes an independent system that must clear supply
against demand on its own, with no buffer, and the price is free to take whatever value clears it — including values
that are absurd by any other standard, because there is no arbitrage to discipline them. This is the cleanest
example in finance of a qualitative change in behaviour produced by a single term in the constraint rather than by
anything about the participants.
</PhysicsBridge>

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **Spot does not mean instant.** Every spot market has a settlement convention measured in days, and the gap
  between trade and settlement is where a surprising amount of Track 6 lives.
- **A futures price is not a forecast.** For a storable asset it is the spot price plus carry, an arbitrage
  relation. Reading contango as "the market expects higher prices" is the single most common commodity error.
- **Fungibility is a matter of degree.** Brent and WTI are both "oil" and are not the same asset; nor is grain in
  Chicago the same asset as identical grain in Rotterdam. The difference is transport, and it has a price.
- **Negative prices are not a glitch.** In April 2020 crude traded below zero because holders of expiring contracts
  faced **delivery** with nowhere to store the barrels. Storage capacity is finite, and when it binds, the cost of
  taking delivery exceeds the value of the oil.
