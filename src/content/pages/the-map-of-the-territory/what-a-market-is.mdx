## IN ONE SENTENCE

A market is any arrangement that lets people who want to trade find out what they will have to pay.

## WHY IT EXISTS

Two people who would both gain from a swap have three problems to solve before it can happen: they have to find each
other, they have to agree on terms, and each has to believe the other will actually deliver. Solve any of them badly
and the trade fails even though both parties wanted it — the characteristic failure being a seller who cannot trade
at *any* price, not because the asset is worthless but because searching is too expensive.

Every market institution is an answer to one of those three problems. Exchanges and brokers attack search by
concentrating interest in one place. Auctions, quotes and order books attack agreement by fixing a rule that turns
stated interest into a transaction. Clearing houses, margin and settlement attack trust by making delivery someone's
enforceable obligation. When a market behaves strangely, one of the three is usually under stress, and the first
useful question is which.

## DEFINITION

A **market** is a mechanism for matching prospective buyers and sellers and determining the terms of their trade. It
requires no physical venue and no continuous operation: a weekly livestock auction, a bilateral phone call between
two bank desks, and a matching engine handling a million messages a second are all markets, differing in their
*mechanism* rather than in their nature.

The mechanism specifies who may participate, how they express interest, how a match is made, and what is published
afterwards. Everything observable follows from it. The **price** is the rate at which a swap happened — and since at
any instant there are several candidates for "the price", the useful habit is to ask which one is meant. The
**quantity** is how much traded; it is not an innocent second coordinate, because the price available to you is a
function of how much you want. Your **counterparty** is whoever is obliged to deliver, and on an exchange you will
generally never learn their name, because a clearing house interposes itself the moment the trade is matched.
**Liquidity** is how easily a position can be turned into cash without moving the price against you.

Finally, every market is either a **primary market** — where the issuer sells the security for the first time and
receives the proceeds — or a **secondary market**, where it changes hands between investors and the issuer receives
nothing. Essentially all observable trading is secondary. The two are linked, because a security that resells easily
is worth more when first issued, and that link is the standard reason to care about market quality at all.

## FORMALLY

A mechanism takes the messages participants send and returns who trades with whom, and at what price. Let buyers
submit a willingness to pay $b_i$ for quantity $q_i$, and sellers an acceptable price $a_j$ for quantity $v_j$.
Aggregate demand and supply at a candidate price $p$ are the quantities whose limit price is at least as favourable
as $p$:

$$
D(p) = \sum_i q_i \, \mathbb{1}\left[b_i \ge p\right], \qquad
S(p) = \sum_j v_j \, \mathbb{1}\left[a_j \le p\right]
$$

A uniform-price *call auction* clears at the price where these cross and trades the smaller of the two quantities
there. This is the mechanism used at the open and close of most equity exchanges, and it is the cleanest definition
of a market-clearing price that exists.

A continuous market computes the same thing incrementally and forever: orders arrive one at a time and each is
matched against the standing interest on the other side immediately, or else joins it. The two mechanisms are asked
the same question and return different answers, because the continuous one is path-dependent. The *sequence* of
arrivals matters, not just the set — which is the entire subject of Track&nbsp;2.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="The mechanism is the interaction term">
The recurring beginner's error is to treat price as a property of the asset, perturbed by noise. It is not. A price
is the output of an interaction rule applied to a population of participants: change the rule and the same people
trading the same asset produce different prices, different volatility, and different tails.

The analogy that pays is with lattice models. The asset supplies the degrees of freedom, the participants are the
ensemble, and the mechanism is the coupling. Asking for the fair price of an asset without specifying the mechanism
is like asking for the magnetisation without specifying the interaction. This is why Track&nbsp;2 spends six pages
on the order book instead of treating it as plumbing — the book *is* the interaction term, and most of the
interesting empirical regularities in Track&nbsp;3 are properties of it rather than of the assets.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **A market is not a place.** Most have no venue at all. The FX market, the largest in the world by turnover, is a
  network of bilateral relationships with no central exchange and no closing bell.
- **"The price" is at least four different numbers** — best bid, best ask, last traded, official close — and they
  routinely disagree. Any claim about "the price" that does not survive substituting each of them is a claim about
  something else.
- **Stocks and flows are not comparable.** Equity market capitalisation is a stock; FX turnover is a flow per day.
  Headlines that rank them against each other are comparing a reservoir with a river.
- **More trading is not more liquidity.** Volume can rise while depth collapses, and in a crisis it reliably does.
  They are different measurements of different things, and the crisis is exactly when the difference matters.
