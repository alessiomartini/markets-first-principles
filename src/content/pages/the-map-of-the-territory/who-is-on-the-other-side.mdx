## IN ONE SENTENCE

Every trade has someone on the other side, and knowing what that someone is optimising tells you more about the
price than any amount of analysis of the asset.

## WHY IT EXISTS

The question "who is on the other side?" is the first thing a professional asks about a trade that looks too good.
If a price is attractive, somebody is choosing to sell at it, and either they know something or they are indifferent
for a structural reason. The two cases have opposite implications.

Structural indifference is common and is where most reliable profits come from: a **pension fund** buying long
bonds because its liabilities demand it, an index tracker selling a name because it left the index, a bank hedging
because capital rules require it. None of these participants believes the price is fair. They are solving a
different problem, and someone willing to take the other side is providing a service they are happy to pay for.

The taxonomy below is worth learning for exactly this reason. It is not trivia — it is the list of possible answers
to the only question that matters when a trade looks free.

## DEFINITION

A **broker** executes as your agent for a commission and does not take the position. A **dealer** becomes your
**counterparty** and warehouses it. A **market maker** is a dealer who quotes continuously on both sides, earning
the **bid-ask spread** and aiming to end flat; its enemy is adverse selection, since whoever is most eager to hit
its quote is disproportionately informed.

A **prop firm** trades its own capital with no clients. Modern electronic prop firms are the dominant market makers
in listed markets: small headcount, extreme investment in latency, no redemption risk. A **hedge fund** pools
outside money under few constraints — it may short, lever and concentrate — and is typically the marginal buyer of
risk, which also makes it a forced seller when leverage is withdrawn.

An **asset manager** invests against a mandate, usually measured against a benchmark. That structure matters more
than any view it holds: deviating from the benchmark is what carries career risk, which is why enormous sums track
indices mechanically. A **pension fund** is distinguished by having liabilities of known shape decades out, so its
risk is the gap between assets and promises, not the volatility of assets alone.

**Retail flow** is orders from individuals. It is prized because it is on average uninformed and therefore
profitable to quote against — the entire economic basis of payment for order flow. Finally, the **CCP** is the
participant you never see: it novates itself between the two sides after matching, so you face it rather than your
actual counterparty.

## FORMALLY

The market maker's problem states the whole ecosystem in one line. Suppose a fraction $\pi$ of incoming orders come
from informed traders who know the true value will move by $\pm\delta$, and $1-\pi$ from uninformed flow. Quoting a
half-spread $s$, expected profit per trade is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{profit}] = (1-\pi)\, s \;-\; \pi\,(\delta - s)
$$

Setting this to zero gives the breakeven spread,

$$
s^\* = \pi \delta
$$

The spread is not a fee; it is the price of adverse selection, paid by uninformed traders to compensate the maker
for losses to informed ones. Three consequences follow immediately, and all three are observed. The spread widens
when informed trading is more likely, which is why it blows out around announcements. Segmenting **retail flow**
away from the exchange raises $\pi$ on what remains, so public spreads widen — the systemic objection to
**internalisation** stated as arithmetic. And a market with no uninformed participants at all cannot be made at any
spread, which is why some markets simply stop.

## PHYSICS BRIDGE

<PhysicsBridge idea="Not a gas of identical particles">
The instinct a physicist brings is to model participants as a homogeneous population and let a mean field emerge.
For most of Track 3 this works well — the stylised facts of prices are robust to who exactly is trading. Here it
fails, and it is worth knowing where the boundary is.

The reason is that the population is *structurally* heterogeneous rather than statistically so. Participants differ
not by a parameter drawn from a distribution but by their objective function: one is maximising growth, another is
matching a benchmark, another is meeting a liability, another is satisfying a capital rule. Averaging over them
does not produce a representative agent, because the constraints do not average — and the trades that look free are
precisely the ones generated by a constraint rather than by a belief. The agent-based models at the end of
Track 5 exist because of this: heterogeneity of objectives is the ingredient that a mean-field treatment cannot
recover.

## COMMON CONFUSIONS

- **"Institutions" is not one group.** A pension fund and a high-frequency prop firm are both institutional and
  have nothing in common in horizon, objective or constraint.
- **A market maker is not obliged to trade with you.** Formal obligations exist on some venues and are weak; in
  stress, quotes widen or disappear, which is exactly when the obligation would matter.
- **Retail is not the dumb money in the way the phrase implies.** "Uninformed" is a technical term meaning the flow
  does not predict the next move. It is a statement about information, not about intelligence.
- **You almost never face your actual counterparty.** The **CCP** novates itself in between, which is why you can
  trade anonymously and why counterparty risk in listed markets is concentrated rather than distributed.
